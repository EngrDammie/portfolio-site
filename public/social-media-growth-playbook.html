01 · Why this document exists

So that a year of posting is a plan rather than a mood.

You have a working site, real projects, and a genuine offer. What is missing is a repeatable way to put that in front of people who need it, every day, without it turning into either a chore or a performance.

This document holds three things: the platform rules that actually changed recently, a set of launch posts you can publish today, and a 365-day calendar of specific angles so you never face a blank page. It also covers paid advertising, because organic reach alone is slow, and because the fastest customers come from being visible where buyers already look.

It is an internal working document. It is excluded from search results, so nothing here will ever be indexed.

How to use this document

Parts I to V are reference: read once. Part III is copy — publish it, then stop looking at it. Part VI is the part you will return to, because that is where interest turns into money. The calendar in section 22 is the tool you will actually keep open.

02 · What “viral” actually buys you

Honesty first, because it changes how you should read everything else here.

You asked for viral posts. I can write strong ones. I cannot make them viral, and neither can anyone who tells you otherwise. Virality is mostly luck, timing and audience size. A post reaching 400 people who all need your work is worth more than one reaching 40,000 strangers.

So this document optimises for enquiries, not for reach. Every recommendation below is filtered through one question: does this bring someone closer to paying you?

The tactics that do not work, and why people sell them anyway

“Comment YES and I'll DM you the template” and “Tag three people who need this” are engagement bait. They generate comments, and platforms have now trained specifically to recognise and suppress them. Your reach goes up on paper and your enquiries go down, because the people who comment “YES” are not buying anything.

Engagement pods — trading likes in groups — are detected, and penalty visibility. Avoid entirely.

What actually works is unglamorous: be specific about one real thing you know, publish it consistently, and be easy to contact. That is the whole strategy.

04 · What you are selling, in one line

Get this wrong and every post you write will sound like marketing.

You are not selling “digital transformation”. You are selling a specific, unglamorous outcome: software that works, that you own, and that someone hands you completely.

  Who you help

Small and mid-sized businesses — in Nigeria, the UK, the US and Europe — whose work still runs through spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads and one person who knows how things work.

  What you remove

The repetitive, manual, error-prone part of a process. The thing that eats someone's week and quietly costs money.

  What they get back

Software that is fast, reliable, offline-capable where it needs to be, and entirely theirs. Source code included, no lock-in, no licence bloat.

Note what is not in there: no years in business, no team, no office, no “leading agency”, no client logos you do not have. The market is full of those and they are the reason a genuinely competent solo engineer gets overlooked. Your lack of those things is the pitch, provided you frame it correctly: you talk to the person doing the work.

05 · What changed in 2026

Verified against current sources, September 2026. Most advice online predates these.

Four changes matter, and two of them invalidate advice you will find on blogs written this year.

ChangeEffectWhat you do about it
External links penalised LinkedIn posts with an outbound link in the body reach roughly 30–60% fewer people than the same post without one. Instagram does not make caption links clickable at all for most accounts. Publish the insight natively and put the link somewhere that does not cost reach. Never post a link with no context attached.
Shares beat likes LinkedIn now ranks on a “depth” score of dwell time, saves and shares. Ten saves beats two hundred likes. On Instagram, DM shares carry the highest weight of any signal. Write things worth keeping — checklists, frameworks, actual numbers. Ask for the save indirectly by being genuinely reusable.
Dwell time is the ranking signal LinkedIn measures real reading time. Fifteen seconds of active reading is reported to earn around 40% more reach. 150–300 words with a strong opening line. A short shallow post that is scrolled past in two seconds is worth less than a longer one that is read.
Generic content is punished Platforms do not detect AI text — they measure behaviour. Templated filler has nothing specific in it, so nobody reads or saves it, so it is suppressed. Every post needs one detail that could only come from your actual experience. This is the single biggest lever you have.
Two pieces of stale advice still circulating widely

“Post at peak engagement times.” Research through 2026 found scheduling does not affect organic reach on LinkedIn. Rank is determined by quality and the behaviour it produces, not by the clock. Post when you can post well and keep to it.

“Hashtags reach.” Instagram's own head has confirmed hashtags do not boost distribution — they categorise. What does work is plain keywords written in the caption, which are reported to drive roughly 30% more reach than a hashtag-heavy post with vague text. Three to five relevant hashtags, and write them into sentences.

06 · LinkedIn

Where your money is most likely to come from. Prioritise this platform.

For business software services, LinkedIn is the highest-value platform per hour spent, and the hardest to grow on. That combination makes it worth the effort.

🔥 Format ranking in 2026

Document carousels lead on combined reach and engagement, image posts follow, then native short video, then plain text. Polls win raw reach but start shallow conversations. Posts containing external links perform worst of anything.

📈 What “healthy” means now

Median organic reach has roughly halved. Expect 2–5% of your audience on a normal post, and 10–20% if your engagement is genuinely strong. A post reaching 200 people from 500 followers is doing its job.

The rules

  • Use your personal profile, not a company page. Company pages now account for roughly 2% of what appears in feeds. Personal profiles generate about five times the engagement of the equivalent company post. Do not build the brand on a company page.
  • Never put your link in the post body. That is the 30–60% penalty. Say “link in the comments” and put it in your first comment, immediately after publishing. Some 2026 sources report the first-comment route is also being dampened, so treat it as “acceptable” rather than “safe”, and always deliver the full value in the post itself.
  • Three hashtags maximum. More reads as spam.
  • No engagement bait. Not “comment YES”, not “tag someone”. Write hooks that earn a reply on substance.
  • First person, narrative structure. Situation, tension, resolution. Reported to beat listicle formats by around 25%.
  • Reply in depth. Comments longer than three words that ask a follow-up question boost distribution. One-word and emoji replies do nothing. This is free reach and almost nobody does it.
  • Spend 10–15 minutes engaging before and after you post. Comment on other people’s work. This is reported as the single highest-leverage habit.
  • Never vanish for more than 30 days. Returning after silence costs around 45% reach on the next post. If you take a break, post something short.
  • Act within 24–48 hours. Reach means nothing unless you reply to everyone who engaged, while they are still looking at the post.
Cadence

Three to five posts a week, every week. Momentum is built by regularity over months and is very hard to recreate with occasional brilliant posts. Three is enough to be sustainable for a solo business and still enough to compound.

07 · Instagram

Where strangers find you. Treat it as discovery, not as a sales channel.

Instagram will introduce you to people who have never heard of you, which no other platform here does reliably. It is also the weakest at converting, because the path to your site is long and awkward.

The rules

  • Caption links are not clickable for most accounts. Only Meta Verified accounts get a small monthly allowance. Put the link in your bio, or use the link sticker on a Story.
  • Reels reach people who do not follow you. Feed posts mostly reach people who already do. If the goal is discovery, it is Reels or nothing.
  • Sends are king. When someone DMs your Reel to a friend, that is the strongest signal in the system. Design content worth forwarding — that is what “share this with the team who still does X manually” means.
  • The first 1.7 seconds decides everything. No logo animation, no intro, no slow build. Open on the most interesting frame or the most surprising sentence.
  • 85% of video is watched muted. Burn in captions. Word-by-word captions outperform static ones.
  • Keep everything in the middle 60% of the frame. The interface covers the bottom quarter and the top strip.
  • Write real keywords into the caption. “how to automate invoice follow-up” as a sentence beats fifteen hashtags.
  • Three to five hashtags, in sentences where possible.
  • Never repost content carrying another platform’s watermark — it is detected and suppressed.
  • Finalise everything before publishing. Editing a caption, hashtag set or cover after posting resets engagement signals.

Set your bio before you post anything

The caption cannot carry your link, so the bio is your only clickable path. Write it to say what you do and who for, not just your name. Something like “AI automations & web apps for businesses drowning in manual work”. Then put the site link below it.

08 · X / Twitter

Best for volume and conversation. Weakest for closing.

X rewards frequency and brevity, and it is where software developers talk to each other. That makes it useful for being known in your field and close to useless for convincing a business owner. Post there for reach and credibility; close elsewhere.

  • 280 characters. Every word must earn its place.
  • The first line is a hook, not a greeting. No “Good morning, everyone!”
  • Post two to five times a day on build days. This is the one platform where volume genuinely helps.
  • Reply to larger accounts in your field. Replies are often seen by more people than original posts.
  • Threads work for explaining something properly. Post the hook, then the thread, and put your link in the final post rather than the first.
  • Do not argue. You will reach an audience of people who enjoy watching fights.

09 · Facebook

Still underrated for local and mid-sized business services.

Facebook is where a lot of small business owners actually are, and unlike Instagram it lets you put a working link in the post body. For businesses with a physical or local presence it outperforms Instagram for actual enquiries.

  • Links in the body work. Use them.
  • Longer posts outperform short ones. Facebook tolerates 2,000+ characters, and the feed rewards completeness.
  • Post in local and industry groups where you are a genuine participant, not a spammer. Read the group rules first.
  • Boost a good post for a few hundred naira rather than building a full campaign. That is the cheapest test available to you.
  • Video, especially short native video, outperforms image posts.

10 · WhatsApp

Your warmest audience and your highest-converting channel. Use it first.

WhatsApp has no public feed, so the mechanics are different from everything else here. You are not broadcasting to strangers — you are working a list of people who already know you, and posting to Status so their contacts can find you.

💬 Broadcast list

Your existing contacts, told clearly that you have started. This is the cheapest first customer you will ever get and you should do it on day one, not week three.

📱 Status

Disappears after 24 hours, needs an image to work at all, and reaches everyone in your contacts’ lists. Post three or four times a week.

For paid work, click-to-WhatsApp ads are the strongest Meta format available to you — covered in section 18. When someone taps an ad they land in a conversation with a pre-filled message, which is the highest-intent action on any platform here.

Set up a WhatsApp Business profile first

Business name as Dammie Optimus Solutions, a catalogue with your three services, an away message, and quick replies for the questions you get repeatedly. Ten minutes of setup, and it makes you look established the moment anyone opens the chat.

11 · Do these two things first

Both take under an hour and both cap your reach if skipped.

One: your link is a workers.dev address

It works, and it is honest. But a workers.dev URL in a bio reads as temporary, and people form an opinion about your technical standard before they read a word of your copy. Buying a domain and pointing it at the same Worker changes nothing in your code — it is a DNS record and one line of configuration.

Until then, lead with what you do rather than the address, and never apologise for it. When you can, this is the single highest-value hour you will spend on marketing.

Two: fix your Instagram bio

The caption cannot carry a clickable link, so the bio is the only path from Instagram to your site. If your bio currently says only your name, Instagram is a channel that cannot convert anyone.

17 · Should you advertise yet?

Not yet. And the reason is arithmetic, not caution.

The learning-phase problem

Meta’s delivery needs roughly 50 optimisation events per week per ad set before it leaves the learning phase. At a modelled cost per lead of around $42 for lead-generation services, 50 events is about $2,100 a week.

Spending less than that keeps your campaign permanently stuck in learning, where costs are unstable and usually higher. Spending more than you have is worse. So at a realistic budget, Meta ads are not yet an efficient way for you to acquire customers.

Do this instead for the first three months: publish consistently, post the launch copy, run the WhatsApp broadcast, put up a small Facebook boost on your best post to test whether paid attention converts at all, and put the time saved into referrals and conversations. Revisit paid properly once you have a handful of real case studies, because that is what makes ads convert.

The exception, worth doing now: Google Search ads. Unlike Meta they do not need a learning phase — you pay per click, and you can start with a very small budget and stop at any time. Intent-based, with no learning overhead.

Cost per lead is close to meaningless on its own

A $28 lead that closes at 8% costs $350 per job. A $90 lead that closes at 40% costs $225. The cheaper lead is the more expensive customer. Always judge paid work by cost per closed job, and keep a record of how many enquiries became projects.

One number that is worth knowing

December is measurably the cheapest month for service-business leads, and almost nobody buys in it. If you ever do run Meta ads, the quiet month is the cheap one.

22 · The 365-day calendar

One specific idea per day, for a year. No blank pages.

Each day gives you a hook — the opening line, which is the part that actually decides whether anyone reads the rest — and a suggested format. The hook is the whole idea in one sentence; the body writes itself once you have it.

Read this before you use it

A year of planned posts is a year of consistency, not a year of execution as written. Some days you will have nothing to say, and some days something will have happened that is far better than anything on this list. Use the calendar to solve the blank page, and replace any entry with something real when you have it.

Real beats scheduled every time. A post about a real problem that happened this morning will outperform a well-written diagnosis you found in a document.

The month structure, and why it changes

Months 1–3 establish you: identity, then diagnosis, then proof. Months 4–7 build credibility: craft, trust, offer, education. Months 8–11 compound it: case studies, objections, community, momentum. Month 12 closes the year honestly. Each month has a character, so if you fall behind, jump to the current month rather than trying to catch up.

How to run the calendar without burning out
  1. One idea, several formats. A good diagnosis becomes a LinkedIn post, an Instagram carousel, and an X thread. You are not writing 365 things; you are writing about 90 ideas across three platforms.
  2. Batch weekly. Two hours on a Sunday to draft the week is the difference between consistency and constant struggle. Never write a post the moment you decide to post it.
  3. Post the same day, roughly the same time. Not for algorithm reasons — scheduling does not affect reach — but so your audience learns when you turn up.
  4. Drop anything you cannot personally vouch for. If a day asks you to claim a result you do not have, skip it. One fabricated number undoes six months of credibility.
  5. Reply for twenty minutes after every post. This is the highest-return habit in the entire document and it costs nothing.

23 · From enquiry to paid work

Attention is not the same as revenue. This is where it becomes one.

Most solo businesses lose more deals in the conversation than they lose in the marketing. A predictable, honest process beats being charming every time.

StageWhat you doTime it should take
Enquiry arrivesYou already send an acknowledgement automatically. Reply again personally within hours, not days. Speed is the single biggest predictor of whether you win.Under 2 hours
First conversationAsk what they do now, what it costs them, and what happens if nothing changes. Do not pitch. Most of the time the conversation reveals the real problem, which is rarely the one in the enquiry.30 minutes

The enquiry form gives you words, not requirements. Quoting off it guarantees you either under-charge for something bigger than described, or over-charge and lose the work to someone cheaper. A thirty-minute conversation is not overhead. It is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

24 · Answering the five objections

The questions that stop people, and honest answers.

  “Why you rather than an agency?”

Answer: An agency has overhead before your project starts, and that cost is in your price. You have almost none. You also do not have to hand your project to an account manager, and you cannot go to seed funding the morning after you mess it up. What you give up is a team of specialists and the ability to absorb very large projects — and I will tell you honestly when you need one.

  “Is this really worth the money?”

Answer: It is worth it if the manual work costs you more than the build does. Work out their hourly rate, work out how many hours a month the task eats, and do the multiplication out loud. Often the honest answer is that it is not worth automating yet — and saying so is how you get the work that is.

  “How long will it take?”

Answer: Give a range with a stated assumption, and be explicit that the assumption is what would change it. “Three to five weeks, assuming you can get decisions within a day” is honest and protective. A single confident date is a promise you cannot keep.

  “What if it breaks?”

Answer: It will, eventually, and that is normal. What matters is that you own the source code and can find someone else, or fix it yourself. Everything is documented, nothing depends on me staying reachable, and there are no licence keys that expire.

  “We’ll think about it.”

Answer: Do not chase immediately. Add value once — a relevant observation about their situation, ten days later. Most “think about it” means the timing is wrong rather than the answer being no, and a timing problem resolves itself. Then stop.

25 · Talking about price

Never apologise for it, and never hide it.

  • Say the number. A range that is wide enough to be dishonest reads as evasion. Give a real range with the assumptions that set it.
  • Price the outcome, not the hours. “This removes about six hours a week of manual entry” lets them judge the cost themselves. You have already done that arithmetic for them.
  • Offer a bounded first phase. A fixed, small first step converts far better than a large speculative one, and it lets you prove yourself before anyone commits properly.
  • Be willing to say it is not worth it yet. This feels like losing a sale and is usually how you win the next three. Nobody remembers the time you declined; everybody remembers the engineer who told them the truth.
  • Never discount to win the work. Reduce the scope instead. Same price, smaller promise, and you keep your rate honest.

27 · What not to do

Every one of these is common advice, and every one of them costs reach.

  • Engagement bait. “Comment YES!” “Tag someone who needs this!” Pattern-matched as low quality and suppressed. Write hooks that earn a reply on substance.
  • Engagement pods or pod tools. Detected, and penalty visibility. The penalty exceeds any boost.
  • Hashtag stuffing. Three to five relevant ones. The algorithms read meaning now, not tags.
  • Links in a LinkedIn post body. Around 30–60% reach reduction.
  • Buying followers or engagement. Wrecks the demographic your content is shown to, which is the only thing reach actually depends on.
  • Posting the same text everywhere. Cross-posting identical content reads as automated and gets filtered. Adapt the hook, keep the idea.
  • Filling a calendar with filler. One honest post about something that happened this week beats six from a list.
  • Claiming results you cannot evidence. Fabricated numbers are the fastest way to lose a technically literate audience, and that audience is the one that hires engineers.
  • Vanishing for a month. Coming back after 30 days inactive costs roughly 45% reach on the next post. Post something small instead.
  • Spending on Meta before the budget supports it. Under the learning threshold it is reliably more expensive than organic.

28 · The first 30 days, as a checklist

Do exactly this, then reassess.

  • Day 1. Send the WhatsApp broadcast. Set up a WhatsApp Business profile. Fix your Instagram bio so it says what you do and carries your link.
  • Day 2. Publish the LinkedIn launch post, with the link in the first comment. Spend fifteen minutes commenting on other people’s posts before and after.
  • Day 3. Publish the Facebook launch post. Post it to one genuinely relevant local or industry group.
  • Day 4. X launch post, then three more short posts about things you actually worked on.
  • Day 5. Instagram launch post with a simple graphic. First Story with the link sticker.
  • Week 2. Start the calendar properly. Three LinkedIn posts, two Instagram, X most days. Batch-draft on Sunday.
  • Week 3. Reply to every single comment and DM. This is where actual enquiries appear, and it is the part everyone quits on.
  • Week 4. Boost your best-performing Facebook post for a few hundred naira. This is the cheapest test of whether paid attention converts at all. Track the enquiries that follow.
  • End of month 1. Count enquiries. Note which platform they came from. Change one thing for month 2. Only one.
  • Month 3 onwards. Add Google Search ads at a small budget when you have at least one case study to point at. Do not touch Meta ads until the budget can support the learning phase.
    • What success actually looks like at 90 days

    Not a viral post. Not ten thousand followers. A handful of real conversations with people who have a problem you can solve. Five serious enquiries in a quarter from a business that is genuinely good at what it does is an excellent result, and it is what this is actually for.
