02 · The honest answer about virality

There is no tactic that guarantees virality. Anyone who promises one is selling something.

"Viral" means content or a product spreads far beyond its existing audience, usually without paid distribution. It is genuinely rare, and its causes are not fully understood even by the platforms whose algorithms produce it. You cannot schedule it, budget for it, or engineer it reliably.

What you can do is build a system that makes spread possible and removes the things that stop it happening:

  1. Be findable. Most "viral" content is found through search, social or word of mouth — and all three routes start with a link someone can actually reach.
  2. Be worth sharing. Things get shared when they save someone time, make them look good, or settle an argument they were having.
  3. Be quotable. Content with a number, a chart, a surprising comparison or a genuinely original study gets cited by others, which is the closest thing to a reliable link engine.
  4. Remove friction from sharing. If a reader cannot share, copy or reference something in one action, most will not.
  5. Compound. The realistic goal is not one viral hit. It is a growing body of work where each new piece makes the next one easier to distribute.
⚠️ The realistic expectation

For a new domain, published by a solo developer, with a modest content output, published benchmark data from a large sample of sites shows a median of roughly 1,200 monthly organic visits in year one, 4,800 in year two and 11,500 in year three (Ahrefs study of 500+ new domains). Not a hype number. Not zero. Those figures are a useful anchor for what "normal progress" looks like, and they are the reason this document is mostly about consistency rather than tricks.

The more useful reframe: you are not trying to go viral. You are trying to become the place that gets found when someone searches for the problem you solved. That is a compounding, measurable, unglamorous process — and it is far more reliable than waiting to be noticed.

03 · What SEO actually is

Three things must happen, in order. Most SEO advice skips the first two.

Search Engine Optimization is the practice of making it more likely that a search engine will show your page to someone searching for a particular thing. The mechanics are simpler than the industry suggests, and the model everyone at Google and Moz agrees on is a three-stage funnel (Moz):

StageWhat happensHow it fails
Crawl A robot (a program, not a person) follows links and downloads your pages. No links reach the page, or the file is too big to fetch.
Index Google stores the page in its search database and decides what it is about. The page is blocked, marked noindex, or has no useful unique content.
Rank For a given query, Google orders the pages it holds. Someone else's page is judged more relevant, more useful or more trusted.

The critical insight is that the first two are not optional, and they are not rankings. A page that is not crawled and indexed cannot rank at all, no matter how good it is. This matters enormously for your site right now, because the failure is mechanical rather than competitive — see 06 and 10.

What Google weighs, roughly in order of importance

  • Meaning — does the page actually contain what the searcher wanted?
  • Quality and originality — is there something here that is not just a restatement of the top ten results?
  • Authority — do other people, sites and systems vouch for this?
  • Usability — does it load quickly and work on the device being used?
  • Context — the words, links and structure around the content that tell a machine what the page is for.

There is no hidden trick, no magic keyword, and no amount of meta-tag tweaking that substitutes for having pages that are reachable, indexable and genuinely worth reading.

II The foundation

07 · Crawlability

Can a robot find the page by following links?

Crawlers discover pages by following links from pages they already know about. A page that nothing links to is effectively invisible until something external points at it or you submit the URL by hand. This is why 10 is the top priority.

The practical requirements

  • Every important page must be reachable within a few clicks of the homepage. Three clicks is a reasonable ceiling for anything you care about.
  • Links must be real <a href> elements. Navigation built only from JavaScript, or buttons that navigate on click without an href, may not be followed reliably by every crawler.
  • Pages must be under reasonable size limits. Google's crawler has a limit of roughly 2 MB for an individual file, with a default total of 15 MB per page. Your homepage is currently about 96 KB, so this is not a problem for you — but it is worth knowing if a future page embeds a large dataset.
  • Don't hide content behind login walls or interaction. Anything requiring a click to reveal is discovered less reliably than content present in the HTML.
Your situation: your homepage is small and fast, which is good. The problem is the opposite of a crawl-budget problem — there is essentially no crawl path at all. Section 10 fixes it with internal links and a footer.

08 · Indexability

Reachable is not the same as stored. Indexing is where most "my site isn't showing up" problems live.

Indexing is the decision to keep a page in the search database and to record what it is about. A page can be perfectly crawlable and still be excluded. The common causes:

CauseWhat it looks likeFix
noindex directivePage is crawlable but absent from resultsSearch the page source for noindex and remove it
Blocked in robots.txtCrawler can see the block, so it never fetches the page to see it was allowedRemove the Disallow line
No unique contentMultiple pages judged duplicates of one anotherGive each page something distinct, or consolidate them
Thin or low-value contentCrawled and assessed, then droppedImprove it, merge it into something better, or remove it
Server errors or timeoutsFetch fails, page never arrivesFix availability and response time

There is a useful asymmetry here. Blocked pages cannot be rescued by quality — if a crawler never downloads the page, it cannot know the content is excellent. And robots.txt and noindex fail in opposite ways: a noindex tag is seen only if the crawler successfully fetches the page, so a page blocked by robots.txt can carry a noindex tag forever without it ever taking effect.

Industry testing suggests that fixing indexing and crawl problems is one of the fastest available wins, with reported uplifts in the region of 15–35% organic traffic within four to eight weeks on sites where such problems were the bottleneck. On a site like yours, that is a live possibility rather than a hypothetical.

How to check: Google Search Console's URL Inspection tool (free, and 46 covers setting it up) will tell you the current index status of any URL on request. Until a property is verified, you can test approximately by searching Google for site:yourdomain.com — crude, but it will show whether a single page has been picked up at all.

09 · Sitemaps: the missing map

A sitemap is a list of every page you want found, in a format machines understand.

A sitemap is an XML file listing every URL that should be indexed, optionally with a last-modified date. It does not directly improve rankings. What it does is remove ambiguity: it tells search engines exactly which pages exist, which is especially valuable on a site where discovery is otherwise difficult.

For your site specifically, this matters more than the general case, because sitemap.xml currently returns 404 Not Found. Without it, and without internal links, the seven documents have two independent discovery problems and neither is solved.

What a sitemap does and does not do

  • ✅ Does tell engines which URLs exist and when they last changed.
  • ✅ Does make newly published pages discoverable faster.
  • ✅ Does give a clear signal of what you consider your real site, as opposed to parameter URLs and duplicate variants.
  • ❌ Does not get a page ranked.
  • ❌ Does not submit itself. Listing a URL in a sitemap does not automatically enqueue it for crawling — that still requires links, or submission through Search Console.
  • ❌ Does not fix a broken site. If pages are blocked or non-existent, a sitemap listing them produces warnings rather than results.
Recommendation: generate a small, hand-maintained sitemap.xml listing the homepage and the seven documents, reference it from 19 via a Sitemap: line in robots.txt, and submit it in Search Console. Approximately 20 minutes of work. Listed as P1 in the backlog.

11 · The workers.dev ceiling

A subdomain is a real limit. This one is structural, not cosmetic.

Your site is served from get-tech-solutions.dammieoptimus.workers.dev. The domain you actually control is dammieoptimus.workers.dev, which is a subdomain of workers.dev — a domain owned by Cloudflare, shared by every Cloudflare Worker user in the world. You do not own it and you cannot register it.

Three concrete consequences, in ascending order of importance:

  1. No authority passes between your projects. When your homepage links to starium-app.dammieoptimus.workers.dev, that is a link to a different site from search engines' perspective. It confers no benefit to you and no benefit to that project. Six strong cross-links that would normally compound into real authority are currently doing nothing.
  2. You cannot build a coherent brand domain. wa.me links, booking links, email addresses and every piece of content you publish will eventually need to reference a memorable address. A long subdomain of a shared platform is not that.
  3. It signals "temporary". Both people and automated systems treat *.workers.dev as a free hosting subdomain. A visitor arriving from a cold source has an immediate, unstated reason to be less confident. This is a trust tax on every acquisition channel that does not already know you.
🚨 Recommendation: obtain a real domain

This is arguably higher priority than any content work. A matching .com (with .ng and relevant ccTLDs if available and affordable) attached to the same Cloudflare Worker costs very little per year, takes an afternoon to configure, and improves credibility, link accumulation and brand coherence simultaneously.

It also makes every other recommendation in this document easier: canonical URLs become meaningful, hreflang becomes possible (36), structured data can reference a real organisation identity (18), and the documents' internal linking starts passing authority where it currently cannot.

Until that is done, one mitigation is available at zero cost: the homepage already describes the business in its title and description. Keeping those accurate and complete is what compensates for the unbranded address. Adding an author or organization meta tag (06) does the same job at a token's cost.

14 · Accessibility as infrastructure

The same markup that helps screen readers helps machines understand the page.

Accessibility is a requirement for a real business, and it has a direct and often underrated SEO consequence: the structural signals that make a page usable with assistive technology — proper heading order, descriptive link text, alternative text, a declared language, labelled controls, sufficient contrast — are largely the same signals that tell a search engine or an AI system what the page contains and how it is organised.

This is not a metaphor. A link reading "click here" tells a screen-reader user and a crawler equally little. A page with a correct H1 → H2 → H3 hierarchy is legible to both. Fixing accessibility tends to improve machine comprehension as a side effect, which is why it is listed here rather than treated as a separate concern.

Status of your existing documents

  • ✅ Declared document language, so screen readers use the correct pronunciation.
  • ✅ Semantic landmark elements and a single H1 per document.
  • ✅ Keyboard-operable theme and print controls with accessible names.
  • ✅ Monospace numerals and tables sized for legibility rather than density.
  • ⚠️ Worth an audit pass: the reading progress bar is visual-only and should carry appropriate ARIA attributes; table headers should include scope; and any purely decorative elements should be explicitly hidden from assistive technology.

A structured accessibility pass over the documents is a small task with two returns at once, and it should be scheduled alongside the metadata work in 06 rather than treated as a separate project.

III Being cited by AI

This part covers what has genuinely changed in the last two years: a growing share of searches now end without anyone clicking a link at all. For a business that sells its thinking and its capability, that shift is more consequential than any conventional ranking change.

15 · Zero-click and AI Overviews

More people are getting their answer without ever reaching a website.

Search engines increasingly answer the question directly on the results page — a summary box, an AI-generated overview, a highlighted answer, a product carousel. The user gets what they needed and never clicks. Industry estimates place zero-click behaviour at roughly 69% of all searches in 2026, up from around 56% in 2024, with the position-one click-through rate reported as falling by about 58% when an AI Overview is present.

⚠️ Read this carefully before panicking

The raw story is "fewer clicks". The accurate story is that the clicks that remain are far better qualified. Traffic referred by AI assistants has been reported to convert at roughly 4.4 times the rate of standard organic traffic, because someone who asked an assistant a detailed question and followed its recommendation has already self-qualified. Fewer visitors, better ones. That is a favourable trade for a business selling considered services, and it is a reason to optimise for citation rather than abandon the pursuit of being found.

There is a second-order effect that matters more for you. If the answer is generated on the results page, then the only way to appear in it is to be one of the sources the system consulted. That makes the question "am I cited?" a new and separate question from "do I rank?", and it makes 16 the most important section in this part.

16 · How answer engines pick sources

A small, selective set of pages wins. Being good enough is not the same as being chosen.

Unlike a search results page which can display a hundred blue links, an AI answer typically cites a very small number of sources — commonly reported in the range of two to seven domains per response. This has an important strategic implication:

The long tail of "good enough" disappears. On a normal results page, being the ninth result still produces clicks. Inside an AI answer, being outside the top handful of consulted sources produces nothing. The competitive bar for inclusion is far higher than the bar for a page ten, and the win, once achieved, is disproportionately large.

Based on current practice and the research available in 2026, sources tend to be selected on:

  1. Machine-readable clarity. Pages whose subject can be identified without interpreting layout or visual context. Clear entities, clear headings, clear claims.
  2. Extractable answer passages. Content that states a specific answer in a self-contained sentence near the top, rather than requiring the reader to assemble it from a page of prose.
  3. Independent corroboration. Mentions, citations and coverage from other credible sources — the strongest single signal in the current literature.
  4. Proximity of evidence to the claim. A number with its source visible in the same block is far more likely to be quoted than a number in a footnote.
  5. Structured data. Machine-readable schemas that state unambiguously what kind of thing the page is (18).
  6. Freshness and clear dates. Systems must decide whether information is current. An explicit, maintained date removes that guesswork.
✅ What this means for a small site

The gate is narrow, but it is not a popularity contest. A solo developer with the only published benchmark data on a topic is a very strong candidate for citation, because there is nothing else to cite. Being the definitive source on a narrow subject is a defensible position that large, generic competitors will not bother to occupy. That is the strategic opening this whole document is built around.

17 · Writing so answers can be extracted

Change how the pages are written, not just what they say.

This is a writing discipline, and it is the cheapest high-impact change available to you. It applies to the documents you already have.

1. Answer the question in the first two sentences

The most reliable pattern in the whole discipline. If a section is titled "What is the crawl budget?", the first sentence should be the answer, not a restatement of the question. Never open by working up to a conclusion; open with the conclusion and then justify it.

2. Make one page per question, not one page per topic

A page attempting to answer forty questions in forty thousand words is difficult for both humans and machines. Split it. Each of the 55 sections in this document is arguably a candidate for its own page if you wanted maximum extractability — which is precisely how programmatic and cluster approaches work, discussed in 22 and 26.

3. Keep claims self-contained

A sentence that quotes a figure should contain the figure, the subject and the year in that one sentence. A figure that only makes sense if you have read the four paragraphs above it will not be extracted, and a fact that is not extracted does not exist for citation purposes.

4. Use tables for comparisons and steps for procedures

Tables are highly extractable and easy for both readers and machines to parse. A thirty-five-row rates table is a good asset. The same information as thirty-five sentences is not.

5. State dates, sources and authorship explicitly

"Verified September 2026", "Source: World Bank", "Nigerian Communications Commission data" — these appear throughout your existing documents already, and they are exactly right. A claim with visible provenance is more citable than the same claim without it. Note that the rel="nofollow"-free, plainly-attributed source link in the pricing report is a model of this.

6. Maintain it visibly

A visible "last reviewed" date that is genuinely kept current is a trust signal, and it resolves the freshness ambiguity that citation systems must otherwise guess at.

Applied to your existing work: the pricing report already does most of this — named sources, dated, tabulated, and a clear verdict up front. The novel does not, and should not: fiction is not competing for factual citation, and its job is different entirely. Keep the disciplines separated by purpose rather than applying one template to everything.

20 · Measuring AI citations

The measurement problem is real, and the honest answer is that no tool is complete yet.

There is no Search Console equivalent for AI citations. Google Search Console reports performance in Google Search; it says nothing about inclusion in AI Overviews, and there is no first-party reporting surface for other assistants. This is a genuine measurement gap, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling access to an incomplete dataset.

What is available, in rough order of usefulness:

  1. Manual prompt panels. Define a fixed list of 20–40 prompts that a real customer would ask, run them monthly across the assistants that matter, and record whether you are cited. Slow, free, completely reliable as a trend line, and entirely under your control. This is the method recommended in current AEO practitioner guidance.
  2. Referral-traffic analysis. Segment analytics by referrer to see how many sessions arrive from AI surfaces. Undercounts substantially, because many clicks are unattributed, but it is a real signal and it is free.
  3. Brand-mention monitoring. Watch for mentions of the brand across the web. This is a leading indicator: as 28 explains, mentions tend to move before citations do.
  4. Commercial monitoring tools. Vendors sell AI-visibility dashboards. They are worth using for trend direction, and worth paying for only after the free methods are established, because their absolute numbers are estimates.
The key methodological point: AI responses are not deterministic. The same prompt can produce different answers, and different assistants disagree. Measure direction and share over a fixed prompt set, never a single query. Detail in 47.

IV Content authority

This part is the engine. The rest is plumbing and distribution. Authority is what compounds, and it is the reason "publish more" is both right and dangerously incomplete — volume without structure dilutes; structure without consistency stalls.

21 · Choose three to five core topics

Breadth defeats depth. Pick a small number of subjects and become genuinely useful in them.

Topical authority means being recognised as a reliable source on a subject. The mechanism is coverage: a reader or a system who lands on one of your pages about a topic should find several others, all consistent, all obviously by the same author with the same standard. One page is an article. Eight pages that interconnect are a body of work, and the second is treated very differently from the first.

The number matters. Publishing across twenty unrelated subjects is the most common way small sites waste their effort, because each new subject starts from zero authority and none of them ever reaches the threshold where pages reinforce each other. Three to five is a realistic range for a solo operator maintaining a site alongside client work.

22 · Topic clusters

One strong page, supported by several specific ones, all linked together.

A topic cluster is a standard structure for building authority: a comprehensive pillar page on a broad subject, surrounded by focused supporting pages on the individual subtopics, with every supporting page linking up to the pillar and across to its siblings. It mirrors how people actually research something — starting broad, then drilling into the specific question that is actually blocking them.

The reported effects are consistent across sources: sites using cluster structure see meaningfully more organic traffic, and rankings hold considerably longer. Moz reports roughly a 25% traffic increase associated with high topical authority; other practitioners report around 30% more traffic and rankings lasting about 2.5 times longer with clusters versus unrelated standalone posts. The precise figures vary — but every source agrees on the direction, and the mechanism is straightforward enough to trust.

23 · Internal linking and cannibalisation

Internal links are how you tell a search engine what matters. Most sites under-use them badly.

External links tell you who vouches for you. Internal links tell you what you consider important, and they distribute authority through your own site. A well-linked cluster raises the floor for every page in it; an island of unlinked pages is invisible no matter how good it is.

Rules that hold up in practice

  • Every supporting page links up to its pillar with descriptive anchor text. "Read the full pricing benchmark report" is informative; "click here" is not.
  • Supporters link to each other where genuinely relevant. Practitioners commonly aim for around five to six contextual internal links per article — enough to form a network, few enough to stay useful to a reader.
  • Link from your strongest pages to your new pages, and not only from the navigation. A link from the homepage or the pillar carries far more weight than a link buried in a sidebar.
  • Use a flat, readable URL structure — /pricing/function-points rather than deep nesting. It signals hierarchy, it is easy to cite, and it survives a future reorganisation.

Cannibalisation: when your own pages compete with each other

If two pages on your site target effectively the same question, Google must choose one, and both may rank poorly. This is a real risk for you specifically, because the hub, the app documentation and the pricing report all touch on some of the same ground.

Three ways to resolve it, in order of preference:

  1. Consolidate. Merge the two into one stronger page. Almost always the best answer, and it removes a maintenance burden too.
  2. Different intent. If both genuinely deserve to exist, make sure they satisfy clearly different needs — one explanatory, one transactional — and cross-link them so the relationship is explicit rather than accidental.
  3. Canonicalise. Point a canonical tag from the weaker page at the stronger one. This is a genuine recommendation for your documents, and it is why the canonical discussion in 06 is not pedantry.

Worth stating for the novel specifically: The Nameless Column is deliberately not part of the commercial topical strategy and does not link into the document cluster. Fiction competing with a pricing report for the same search terms would be a mistake, and keeping them structurally separate is the right call. It can be published, promoted and shared as a creative project on its own terms.

25 · Refresh before you write

The cheapest traffic available is on pages you already own.

There is a widely replicated finding that updating content which already has search history produces a large share of the available traffic gain for a small fraction of the effort — commonly reported as roughly 43% of the traffic gain at 23% of the time cost compared with writing new material. An existing page has something new content does not: a search history, accumulated links, and a known position to improve from.

Before writing anything new, run this checklist against what already exists:

  1. Which existing pages have impressions but weak click-through? Those are title and description problems, not content problems. The cheapest possible win.
  2. Which pages rank on page two or three for terms they nearly win? Small additions to an already-relevant page frequently move these.
  3. Which pages are out of date? The pricing report's currency assumption and the research dates are the kind of detail that ages visibly. Freshness is a visible quality signal.
  4. Which two pages compete with each other? Consolidating them (23) often produces a larger gain than any single new page.
  5. What question does every page fail to answer? That gap is your next piece of content, and it will be far better targeted than anything chosen in the abstract.

This is also the cheapest AI-visibility work available. Refreshing a page that has already been crawled and assessed means you are improving something the system already has a relationship with, rather than asking it to discover something new from nothing.

26 · Programmatic SEO, and when not to

Generating many pages from structured data. Powerful, and a well-known way to do serious damage.

Programmatic SEO means producing pages from a template plus a dataset — a page per city, per integration, per tool comparison, per use case. Done well it scales enormously. Done badly it is the fastest route to having a site ignored, because the output is thin, repetitive, and adds nothing a reader would not get from a hundred near-identical pages.

When it is justified

  • You have a genuine structured dataset behind the pages. Real, specific data — not a template filled with invented values.
  • Each page serves a distinct intent that the others do not satisfy.
  • You can keep the dataset fresh. A stale generated page is worse than none, because it actively misleads.
  • You have first-party insight to add — the thing that makes a page authoritative rather than merely indexed.

When it is not

  • To produce volume. Volume without distinct value is a liability.
  • Using AI to generate thin text at scale, regardless of how well templated the structure is. This is the single most common programmatic SEO failure and it is entirely avoidable.
  • Before the foundation is in place. Generating thousands of undiscoverable pages multiplies the problem in 10 rather than solving it.

What you already have that qualifies

Your pricing report is, in effect, a hand-built prototype of this. It takes a structured dataset — project types, feature sets, regional rates, estimated hours — and presents it systematically for comparison. A legitimate scaled version would be a page per project type per region, each containing that combination's actual figures rather than a placeholder. The data exists; only the presentation layer is missing.

The sequencing advice is firm: establish the topic clusters and internal linking first, and only then consider scaling pages. Automation applied to a site whose structure is broken produces a great deal more broken.

28 · Branded mentions beat raw backlinks

The link-building finding that matters most in 2026 is not about links.

A study by Ahrefs reported a notably strong correlation between branded web mentions — the brand or founder's name appearing in text on other sites — and visibility in AI Overviews. In the reported data the correlation sat around 0.664 for mentions against roughly 0.218 for traditional backlinks, on the order of three times stronger. The interpretation is not that links stopped mattering for conventional search; it is that being named is what gets a source consulted by an answer engine, and mentions are far cheaper to acquire than links.

This produces a practical shift in how authority should be pursued:

  1. Get named. Be mentioned by name in articles, podcasts, newsletters, community threads and Slack or Discord discussions, in a context where it is clear who you are. This is achievable without asking for anything.
  2. Be attributable. For a mention to become a citation, a system has to be able to resolve "Dammie Optimus Solutions" to a specific entity with a specific body of work. That resolution is far easier if the same name, the same description and the same written profile appear consistently across many sources — and considerably easier again if that is backed by the structured data in 18.
  3. Then convert mentions into links where the relationship allows it, without asking. Most people who mention you are happy to link you and simply were never asked.
Why this is a genuinely good position for you: the brand is a person's name, which is inherently more memorable and more attributable than a generic company name. "Dammie Optimus" appearing in a technical discussion is straightforwardly resolvable. That is a real asset and it is worth being deliberate about it.

29 · Digital PR

Earning coverage rather than buying links. In 2026 it is the most effective channel.

Digital PR is the practice of getting mentioned in publications that already have an audience, rather than purchasing links. In practitioner surveys, digital PR is consistently rated among the most effective channels — 48.6% of SEO professionals in one survey ranked it first, ahead of guest posting, outreach and link buying.

The reason is straightforward: a mention in a relevant publication is simultaneously a citation for search, a citation for AI systems, a distribution channel, and evidence of credibility. It is the only activity that serves all four objectives at once.

How to do it without a PR team or budget

  • Lead with the research, not the business. "A 28-chapter novel about building a platform" is a story a publication can run. "Hire me for your app" is not.
  • Target publications that serve the audience, not the whole internet. A small, relevant, genuinely-read newsletter beats a large general one that never opens. See 31.
  • Make yourself easy to quote. Provide a short written summary, a clear statistic with its source, a quotable line, and a single image. Journalists and writers reuse material that requires no work to restate.
  • Submit to roundups and "best of" lists. These are the mechanism by which most small businesses first get coverage, they are predictable, and the deadline structure makes them easy to plan.

34 · Local SEO and Google Business Profile

The highest-leverage local channel, and completely unused.

A Google Business Profile is a free listing that makes a business appear in Google Maps and in the local results shown for location-based searches. It is consistently among the highest-return actions available to a small local business, and it appears to be entirely absent from your current setup.

For a business that sells considered professional services, a local profile serves a specific purpose: it converts a search like "software developer" plus a city into a credible, contactable business. The mechanics are unglamorous and reliable:

  1. Create and fully complete the profile. Category, services, service area, hours, contact details, website. Incomplete profiles are filtered out of local results.
  2. Use a real, consistent business name. Do not add keywords to the name field. It is a common tactic and a reliable route to a suspension.
  3. Add real photographs. Of the work, the office, the process. Not stock imagery.
  4. Ask genuinely satisfied clients for reviews, and respond to every one. This is the single largest driver of local ranking and of local trust, and it is entirely within your control.
  5. Post periodically. Updates about projects, insights and results. This feeds the listing and provides material for the algorithm to judge.
  6. Ensure the name, address and phone number are identical everywhere they appear online — your site, the profile, directories, social accounts. Inconsistency here actively damages both local ranking and the entity resolution discussed in 28.

One honest caveat: a Google Business Profile is designed for businesses with a physical location customers visit. For a remote-only solo operator the fit is imperfect, and it is worth confirming current eligibility rules before investing time. 36 · Multilingual with hreflang

Telling search engines which language and region a page is for.

An hreflang attribute tells search engines which version of a page to show to which audience. It is how a site serves an English version and a Nigerian Pidgin version without either being penalised as duplicate content, and it is also how you target multiple countries correctly.

It is, however, firmly later-stage work, for one decisive reason and one practical one.

  • Decisive: hreflang requires stable, absolute URLs on a domain you control. A workers.dev address is not a viable base for a multilingual international structure, and building one there would mean rebuilding it later.
  • Practical: it multiplies your content production and maintenance. Three languages means three times the translation work, three times the review, three times the drift as the English original is updated. Starting before the English version is stable multiplies an unfinished foundation.

The recommended sequence

  1. Publish one strong, complete English version of a page.
  2. Confirm it has real organic traffic before translating anything.
  3. Obtain the real domain (11).
  4. Translate only the pages with proven demand, and treat the translation as a genuine adaptation rather than a mechanical substitution — terms, examples and currency should be localised, not transliterated.
  5. Add hreflang annotations and x-default, and verify in Search Console.
  6. Only then consider additional languages.

The dialect question is worth a sentence of honesty. Nigerian Pidgin has a large online audience and is poorly served in written form, which means content in it is comparatively scarce and correspondingly more distinctive. For a technical audience, however, it works best as a supplement — a Pidgin summary or explainer pointing to the full English technical content — rather than as a replacement. Attempting technical depth in Pidgin risks communicating less precisely with an audience whose most valuable searches are the technical ones.

You should not try to be found equally by everyone. The recommendation is to establish Nigeria as a genuine, visible home market, and let international discovery follow from the strength of the underlying work rather than from separate targeting efforts.

39 · The funnel from visitor to customer

Traffic is an intermediate. It is not the objective.

Almost every optimisation decision reduces to one question: does this move a real person closer to hiring you? A useful mental model has four stages, and each has a different conversion rate. Diagnosing which stage is actually leaking is far more valuable than any traffic increase.

41 · Offers that actually convert

Traffic is worthless without a clear, low-friction reason to act.

The gap between "someone read my website" and "someone contacted me" is usually an offer problem, not a traffic problem. A few changes consistently matter more than any amount of additional search visibility:

  1. Make the next step obvious and singular. One primary action on each page. Right now a visitor can go to a document, a calculator, a booking link, six other projects or WhatsApp. That is a choice, and choices reduce action. Pick one primary action per context and make it genuinely prominent.
  2. Lower the commitment of the first step. A 30-minute scoping conversation is a much smaller ask than "start a project". Make the low-commitment option the default.
  3. Show the price or a range. The pricing report has already done the hardest intellectual work here. A prospect who has read it arrives pre-sold on your process and pre-qualified on fit. That is an enormous advantage, and it is largely being left on the table.
  4. Make the offer specific to the reader. A page about mobile app development should lead to a conversation about mobile app development, not to a generic contact form.
  5. Respond quickly. Response time is one of the strongest predictors of whether an enquiry converts. For a market where the channel is WhatsApp, this means being available, and the cost of a missed enquiry is very high relative to the cost of being reachable.
  6. Remove friction from the enquiry itself. A form asking for eleven fields before a conversation can start loses more enquiries than any pricing objection. Ask for the minimum, and let qualification happen in conversation.

None of this is SEO, and all of it sits upstream of the revenue that justifies the SEO. It is listed here because a growth plan that only addresses visibility produces traffic that goes nowhere.

42 · The calculator as a lead magnet

You have the best lead magnet on the site and it is probably not being used as one.

The project estimator is a genuine free tool that solves a real problem at the exact moment someone is considering spending money. Strategically this is more valuable than it looks:

  • It builds trust before the first conversation. It demonstrates competence concretely rather than claiming it, and it does so in the visitor's own scenario.
  • It is a citation candidate. Tools are persistently linkable as a resource. Each link is a small, permanent acquisition channel.

What would make it work much harder

  1. Make the result page a landing page. It should show the estimate, explain what drives it, and offer one clear next step. Currently it is a number and nothing else.
  2. State the assumptions. The pricing report's currency finding is exactly this — a visitor should be able to see the rate assumption and the basis of the estimate, not just the total.
  3. Allow a detailed estimate to be saved or shared as a link, so the visitor can send it to a partner or an investor. Shared links bring in new visitors who never would have arrived — the mechanism described in 28.

A tool that is trusted because it is honest is worth more over time than one that optimises a short-term conversion number.

47 · Tracking AI citations over time

There is no dashboard for this. Build the simplest one that works.

Because no first-party AI visibility reporting exists, the method is to create it:

  1. Write 20–40 prompts a real customer would actually ask. Not keywords — questions in full sentences. "How much does it cost to build a small business website in Nigeria", "what is the best way to estimate the price of a web app", "who is a reliable software developer in Nigeria". Include branded, unbranded and competitor-comparison prompts.
  2. Fix the list and never change it. A panel you edit quarterly measures nothing. Add new prompts in a separate, separately-tracked group.
  3. Run each prompt across the assistants that matter to your audience once a month. Record whether you are cited, whether you are mentioned, and roughly where you appear.
  4. Record the share cited, not the individual results. AI answers are non-deterministic, so a single query tells you almost nothing. Thirty prompts sampled monthly gives a usable trend line.
  5. Pair it with mention monitoring, since mentions lead citations (28).

Expect the panel to be boring for several months. That is normal and is not evidence of failure — it reflects how long entity establishment takes. Its value is entirely in the trend line, and it should never be reported as a headline number.

48 · Not fooling yourself

The most common way a growth plan fails is by measuring the wrong thing confidently.

These are the specific ways this plan could be misread, and the guard against each:

  • Traffic that never converts. A novel can generate substantial readership and no revenue. Judge by enquiries, always.
  • Your own traffic. You visit your own documents constantly, and documentation pages attract exactly that. Exclude your own visits before drawing any conclusion.
  • Ranking a term nobody searches for. Position one on a query with no volume is not an achievement. Check the impression count behind it.
  • Impressions without clicks. Rising impressions with a flat click-through rate usually means your titles and descriptions need work — a cheap, invisible fix.
  • Comparing against other people's years. Comparing your month six with someone else's month thirty-six is the fastest route to abandoning a plan that is working exactly as expected. Use the 51 baselines, not someone else's screenshots.
  • Confusing a spike with a trend. A single viral day is not a strategy. The question is what the trailing three-month average does.
  • Optimising for the metric instead of the outcome. Improving a click-through rate by writing misleading titles is a well-known failure mode. If the number improves and enquiries do not, the titles are lying.
The discipline worth adopting: review the five metrics in 45 monthly, write three sentences about what changed and why, and change one thing at a time. A monthly written note, kept for a year, will teach you more about what works on your site than any amount of theory in this document.

54 · Glossary

Every technical term used above, in plain English.

AI crawler
A program run by an AI company that fetches web pages to feed them into retrieval and answer systems. Distinct from search crawlers.
Algorithm
The set of rules a platform uses to decide what to show and in what order. Google's algorithm decides rankings; a social platform's algorithm decides what appears in a feed.
AEO / GEO
Answer Engine Optimisation and Generative Engine Optimisation — being the source cited inside AI answers, as opposed to occupying a position on a results page.
Backlink
A link from one website to another. Traditional ranking still values these, particularly from relevant sources.
Canonical URL
The single preferred address for a page, declared so that search engines do not treat its duplicates as separate pages.
Crawl budget
How much crawling a site is permitted or worth spending. Largely a large-site concern; not a constraint for a site this size.
Crawlability
Whether a bot can reach a page by following links from pages it already knows.
Digital PR
Earning coverage and mentions in publications that already have an audience, rather than buying links.
E-E-A-T
Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust — the framework for judging whether content deserves to be shown, and who is behind it.
Entity
A distinct real-world thing — a person, a company, a product — that a system can recognise and connect to other information about.
Front-end matter
The content and structure of a page itself: headings, text, links, images. As distinct from technical infrastructure underneath.
Hreflang
An HTML attribute declaring which language and region a page is intended for, so the right version is shown to the right audience.
Impression
A display of your page in search results. Impressions without clicks usually mean your title and description need work.
Index / indexing
The search engine's stored copy of a page. A page must be indexed before it can possibly rank.
Internal link
A link between two pages on the same site. These distribute authority and tell search engines what you consider important.
JSON-LD
A format for embedding machine-readable structured data in a page using the schema.org vocabulary.
Lead magnet
Something of genuine value offered in exchange for attention or contact details — a tool, a report, a guide.
Long-tail keyword
A longer, more specific search phrase. Lower volume, but far more specific intent and usually easier to win.
Meta description
The short summary shown beneath a page title in search results. It does not directly affect ranking but strongly affects click-through.
Meta tag
A line of HTML in a page's <head> giving a crawler information about the page, such as its description or language.
Noindex
An instruction telling search engines not to include a page in results, used when a page should be reachable but not ranked.
Open Graph
Metadata controlling how a link appears when shared on social platforms — the title, description and preview image.
Pillar page
A comprehensive page on a broad subject, supported by focused pages on its subtopics. The centre of a topic cluster.
Programmatic SEO
Automatically generating many pages from templates and structured data. Powerful when each page has genuine distinct value; damaging when it does not.
Reciprocal links
Two sites linking to each other. Considered a manipulation if the arrangement exists primarily to influence rankings.
Referrer / referral traffic
Visitors arriving from another site. In analytics, the referring site is recorded so source can be understood.
Robots.txt
A file at the root of a site giving instructions to crawlers. Also a valid place to declare a sitemap.
Schema
See JSON-LD and structured data — a machine-readable description of what a page is.
Search Console
Google's free tool for verifying a site and reporting how it performs in search, including indexing and Core Web Vitals.
Semantic
Related to meaning rather than keywords. Modern search works on meaning — which words and concepts a page is genuinely about.
Sitemap
An XML file listing the pages on a site that should be discovered and indexed.
Structured data
Machine-readable markup describing what a page is, so systems do not have to infer it.
Topical authority
Being recognised as a reliable, comprehensive source on a subject, demonstrated through connected, consistent coverage.
Topic cluster
A pillar page plus supporting pages on subtopics, all interlinked. The standard structure for building authority.
Zero-click
A search where the answer is shown on the results page and the user never clicks a result. Now a large share of all searches.
