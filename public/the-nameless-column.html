A Novel of Brightwork

The Nameless Column

A lone builder at the edge of the world builds a machine that shines — and finds that the thing measuring his work has never heard of the people he built it for.

On the grey coast of Amara, when a problem is genuinely solved, the solution gives off light. The Tally comes to measure it. A Brightwright is paid by the value of his glow.

Dammie builds alone at the Ninth Wharf, and he builds well. He also builds for people the Tally has no column for — a shift worker on two bars of signal, a factory inspector with a paper log, a field agent on a night bus. He is paid, generously, for none of it.

Front matter A note to the reader

Before you begin

This is a work of fiction, but every strange thing in it is standing in for something that really happened.

There is no such thing as light that rises from a solved problem. There is no Assessors' Tally, no brightwork, no Ninth Wharf. Those are inventions, and they are here to make something true easier to see.

What is true is this: a young man in West Africa built a single website, and on that website he put a small machine that does arithmetic. He had spent years before that building tools for real people with real problems — a factory where the record was kept on paper and the silos emptied themselves in the dark; a shift worker on two bars of signal who was losing a month of pay over a miscounted day; a field agent with a playbook buried in a chat group. Then he wrote down some numbers for what his work was worth, and he got them badly wrong.

That is the whole book. A man builds something that works, and then finds out the instrument he used to measure it was broken, and that the instrument was broken in a way that suited everybody except him.

To keep the two worlds honest, each chapter ends with a Field Note — a small aside that tells you what was actually happening, in the plain language of a person who was there. If you never read a single Field Note, you will have read a novel. If you read nothing else, the Field Notes alone will teach you more about building software than most courses.

One last thing. Everything in this book that is dark, difficult, and frightening happened to a real person who then fixed it and kept going. There is a lot of that here. It is worth noticing how little of it reads as heroism at the time.

Act One · The Ash Chapter One

The Boy Who Mined Ash

The first thing Dammie learned was that not all light is worth anything.

He was nine, or ten, or eleven. The Ninth Wharf at Amara did not keep its records the way the city did, and nobody had ever thought to start one for him, so the exact figure was lost the way most figures on that coast were lost: not destroyed, simply never written down. He had worked out by then that the glow which rises from a finished thing comes in two kinds.

There was the bright kind. It came off a thing that did what it was supposed to do. A pump that moved water. A lamp that stayed lit through the night. A door that latched when it was supposed to. That light climbed. It wanted to be seen. Grown men would stop what they were doing to watch it go up.

And there was the ash kind. It came off a thing that only looked like it worked. A pump that ran dry after four minutes. A lamp that dimmed at dusk and nobody mentioned. A door that swung shut and left the latch bent a half-centimetre out of true. That light did not climb. It settled. It went grey around the edges and pooled on the ground, and — this was the part that mattered — you could not tell it from ordinary dirt until it was much too late to matter.

By the time anyone on the wharf started calling him a Brightwright, he could tell them apart in his sleep.

The Ninth Wharf was the poor end of the harbour. Three cranes, a salt shed, a customs house with a leak in the roof that four generations of men had decided was a feature, and a long tin shed at the far end where the Assessors did their work. Beyond the wharf the water went out flat and grey for eleven kilometres, and the far shore was a rumour that everybody agreed on and nobody had walked to.

His booth was six metres of lean-to against the salt shed. His tools were a torch, a steel rule, a hand drill he had to wind with his wrist, and a notebook his grandmother had ruled into columns with a nail. She had used it for forty years to keep track of who owed whom what at the market, and when she died she left it to him with the columns still full, and he understood years later that she had been running the most important instrument on the coast and doing it in an exercise book.

✦ ✦ ✦

The first thing he ever finished was a latch.

It was a small, graceless thing — a length of spring steel, a plate, four bolts — and it took him most of a wet week. The old one on the salt-shed door had been bent since before he was tall enough to reach the handle, and every man on the wharf had learned to shoulder the door rather than trust the latch.

He fitted it, and he stood back, and he waited for the light the way you wait for weather.

The light came. It was not much. But it came straight up out of the steel and went about four feet into the air and stopped, and the whole shed had to look at it.

An Assessor had come to the wharf twice in his life. The first time, years later, when Dammie was nineteen and had replaced the salt-shed pump with something he had built himself out of a bicycle gearbox and an unreasonable amount of patience. That pump had pulled eight hundred litres an hour and it had not stopped once in the four years before the salt season, and the light off it had been so bright that a man from the capital had walked the length of the wharf to look at it.

The second time the Assessor came, Dammie was twenty-two, and the assessor did not walk the length of the wharf. She walked the other way. She came to his booth, looked at his notebook, and asked him what he thought a piece of his work was worth.

He told her. He had a number in mind, and it was not a greedy number, and he was quietly proud of it.

She wrote something down. Then she looked at him for slightly longer than the question required, in a way he would think about for the next several years, and said:

The Tally is not a market. It is a record. It records what we have seen, and it cannot see everything, and it has never once pretended otherwise. You have not been undervalued. You have been unread.

Then she paid him, and she left, and he stood in the six metres of lean-to with a number in his hand that was less than a third of what he had asked for, and told himself for about six years that the gap was the price of not arguing.

It was not the price of not arguing.

And his very first fix, before any of the web work, was replacing a bicycle-gearbox-driven pump at a salt depot on the wharf. The instinct the novel gives him in chapter one — build it small, build it so it works, build it for a specific person at a specific place — is the same instinct behind all five of his real projects.

Act One · The Ash Chapter Two

What the Paper Missed

He learned the habit of looking at the record first, in a room where nobody wrote things down.

The detergent plant at Rafi Junction was not a large plant, but it was a busy one, and it ran three shifts, and it made a powder that went eleven hundred kilometres inland in sacks.

Dammie went in on the quality side, because that was where a boy with no qualifications and a grandmother's notebook could be useful without anyone asking questions. He checked sacks. He weighed them. He wrote down what he found in a hardback ledger with a rubber band around it, and then he wrote the same information again in a second hardback, because the first one was the official one and the second one was the one nobody checked.

Six months in, he noticed something that he could not make fit into the ledger.

The silos were emptying at night.

Not all of them. Not obviously. Nothing alarm went off, because nothing on the floor was instrumented for it. But a silo that should have been full would be a third down in the morning, and the powder would be on the floor, and the floor would be swept, and by the time the day shift came in there was a clean concrete apron and a note that said spillage, cause unknown.

He wrote it up. Nineteen times in six months. Nobody did anything, because nobody could act on nineteen pieces of paper with no number attached to them.

That was the real lesson, and it took him years to fully understand it. It was not that people did not care. It was that the record was not in a form that could be acted on. The plant was not lying. It was simply describing itself in a language that no decision could be built out of.

Machines stopped, too. Not loudly. A machine would slow down over a week, and then a shift, and then it would jam, and it would be fixed, and the fix would be a new belt and forty minutes of downtime, and the downtime never made it into any number that anybody in an office ever saw.

And waste. Vast, silent, compounding waste — carton and laminate off the line, weighed by eye, carried out by a man with a barrow who was in a hurry. Nobody knew the number. You cannot improve what you have not measured, and nobody had ever measured it, and so the plant had cheerfully gone on paying for its own inefficiency in sack after sack for nineteen years without one manager suspecting that anything was wrong.

The plant was not lying. It was describing itself in a language that no decision could be built out of.

And underneath all of it, the thing he would later realise was the actual disease.

Managers made decisions from memory.

Not out of laziness. Out of necessity. There was no other information available, so a human being did what human beings do: they held a picture in their heads, and the picture was built from impressions and arguments and whatever the last person had said at the end of a long shift. That picture was not wrong in any way you could point to. It was just not a measurement. Nobody could check it, so it became the truth by default, and everyone in the room became an expert on a subject on which there was no expertise.

Dammie watched this happen for a year. Then he went home and started building something in his grandmother's exercise book that nobody had asked for.

✦ Field note · what actually happened

This chapter is the real reason the Starium project exists. Dammie did work in quality control at a detergent factory, and the problems described here — silos draining silently, machine stoppages never recorded, waste weighed by eye, managers deciding from memory — are the ones he named in the project's own case study, almost word for word.

He never got to fix them. The system he built has twenty-one modules and it runs in a test environment, awaiting management sign-off. That part is true and worth saying plainly: the brightest thing in the story is still waiting for permission.

Act One · The Ash Chapter Three

A Small Light in the Dark

He learned that the hardest problems are the ones where the user cannot afford the obvious answer.

The man in the shift-apparel shop in Amara was called Emeka and he was not, at first, a problem Dammie intended to solve. He was a problem he intended to fail to solve, which is a different thing, and which takes longer.

Emeka worked a rotating roster. Two days on, two days off, two days on. So did half the security detail on the coast. The arithmetic that mattered to him was not complicated, but it was unforgiving, and it was this:

He was losing a month of pay over a single miscounted day.

Vouchers in that part of the world are paid on a cycle. You worked the twentieth of one month through the nineteenth of the next. If you missed a shift, the deduction landed on the cycle currently running, and it did not wait for you to explain. If the company paid you for the previous cycle — which they always did, because that is how money actually moves — then a deduction in the wrong place would not show up as a deduction. It would show up as a shortage three weeks later, on a day you had not worked and could not argue about, on a ledger that a supervisor had written by hand and would not revisit.

So Emeka had a notebook too. Different notebook, worse notebook — a payslip folded into a pocket, a biro with a chewed cap, and a piece of paper with a grid drawn on it that he had filled in and rubbed out and filled in so many times it had gone soft.

Dammie asked to see it. He read it in about ninety seconds, and then he went and sat on a bollard in the sun for a while, because there was a period there where he needed to sit down.

The apps that could do this all cost more than Emeka made in a month. Or they needed a login, a subscription, a connection, an account he did not have, a support line that was open during working hours — which for a man on nights is never.

✦ ✦ ✦

And there was the other thing. The thing that decided the entire shape of what Dammie built.

Emeka had one bar of signal, in a staff room at the back of a depot, in a city where the signal worked fine in some districts and not at all in others. And a modern app that needed a server would have been worth precisely nothing to him at the moment he needed it — which was in the staff room, on a break, at eleven at night, when the maths went wrong.

So Dammie built the answer to a question nobody had asked, which was:

What is the most useful thing you can put on one device, that works with no network at all?

He built it as a single file. Not a small application — literally one file, which you could send to somebody by message and they could open it, and it would work, and it would go on working if the tower went down for a week.

All the arithmetic happened on the device. Nothing was sent anywhere. The record lived in the phone. And when the phone was cleared, or lost, or dropped in water — and Emeka's phone had been dropped in water twice — the record was still exportable, in a form that could be printed, argued over, and handed to an accountant.

It took eleven weeks. It had one file and it had no server and it cost Emeka nothing.

The light that came off it was not very large. It was the size of a matchhead, and it lasted about four seconds, and it was the first time in Dammie's life that the glow off a finished thing had made somebody cry.

He did not keep it. He gave it away. It was a gift, and gifts do not enter the Tally, and he did not think about that at the time because there was no reason to. There was no reason at all.

✦ Field note · what actually happened

This is Rafa Voucher, a real and live project. The one-file, zero-backend, offline-first architecture is exactly as described. So is the voucher cycle running from the twentieth to the nineteenth, the 2-2-2 rotating roster, the attribute of a missed shift landing against the wrong cycle, the local storage, and the export to a printable, arguable format.

He also built the Android version. The entire product — attendance, missed days, streaks, voucher cycles, payouts, theme, export — is one HTML file. It has never needed a server, and it has never sent anybody's data anywhere.

Act One · The Ash Chapter Four

The Tally Comes to Amara

For four hundred years the light had been gathered by hand. Then somebody built a shed, and a rule, and a decision.

The Tally had been in the capital for four hundred years, and in that time it had done exactly one thing, and it had done it extremely well.

It measured brightwork.

Every finished thing on the coast, every time, was carried — or rolled, or dragged, or reconstructed on site — into a long tin shed, and set on a bench, and read. An Assessor would look at it for between four minutes and an hour. Then they would declare a Value, and the Value would be recorded, and the Brightwright would be paid a percentage of it, and the ledger in the capital would grow by one line.

The method itself was almost embarrassingly simple, and Dammie came to love it for that reason. The Assessors did not look at how much work a thing had taken. They looked at how much it would do, and how many people it would do it for, and how long it would keep doing it. A thing that did one thing well and kept doing it for ten years was worth more than a thing that did six things adequately for a fortnight. The system was, in its way, the sanest thing he had ever encountered.

Then the capital built something new, and the meaning of the word place changed.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Bureau of Works had needed a place to keep its own records, and it did not want to pay for a building, so it hollowed a long trench in the high ground outside Amara and lined it with a substance that could hold a signal without losing it. It was not a warehouse and it was not a market. It was a Requisition Store — a place from which anything, from anywhere, could be called for, and would arrive, and would keep arriving, at a cost so far below the old hand-carried system that the entire economy of the coast quietly reorganised itself around it within a decade.

Dammie understood what had happened to the Assessors on the first day the store went live. He watched a woman he had worked beside for six years stand in the middle of a wharf, holding a bracket, trying to work out what she was supposed to do now.

The Tally still measured. But the things it was measuring had started arriving from everywhere, and the bench in the tin shed could only take so many, and the entries that came through the Requisition Store were not weighed by a person at all.

They were weighed by a rule.

That was the whole revolution, and it took about nine minutes to explain to Dammie and he has never entirely forgiven it for being so simple.

The rule, as Dammie first heard itA thing of a given size, made for a given purpose, to be used by a given number of people for a given length of time, has a certain value. The rule does not ask how long it took. The rule does not ask how hard you worked. The rule does not ask what it was worth to you. The rule asks what the thing is. Everything else is argument.

And here was the part that would take him another nine years to fully understand, so he wrote it down at the time and read it back years later with a kind of horror he still cannot name cleanly:

The rule was not wrong.

That was the cruelty of it. The rule was a genuinely good instrument. It was consistent, it was impartial, it did not care who built the thing, it had never once been bribed, and it was completely, catastrophically reliable at measuring exactly one category of value in the entire world.

The other categories simply did not appear on the form.

✦ Field note · what actually happened

The Requisition Store is Cloudflare. The hand-carried Tally is the old world of freelance work — a person with a rule of thumb and a reputation. The rule that replaced it is a small, honest, completely deterministic pricing function reading its numbers out of a data file. It is also entirely consistent: give it the same three inputs and it will give the same answer every time, on any machine, in any country, for ever.

That consistency is exactly why the problem in this chapter is not a bug. A thing that is deterministic is easy to trust and very hard to argue with, which means when the inputs are wrong, nothing in the system will ever tell you.

Act One · The Ash Chapter Five

The Wrong Column

The shape of the mistake is always the same. It is never that a number is too small. It is that a number is in the wrong column.

Dammie began keeping his own ledger in the year he turned twenty-four, and he did it for the same reason every Brightwright on the coast did it: because the Tally's entry for a piece of work was a summary, and summaries lose the details you need in order to argue.

He had four columns. He ruled them himself, with a nail, in a new notebook.

Dammie's ledger, page oneWORK | WHAT IT DID | WHO IT WAS FOR | WHAT I WAS PAID -----------------+--------------+-----------------+----------------- shift arithmetic | one file | Emeka, nights | nothing field manual | 24 guides | agents, 2G | nothing reading plans | 3 apps | me, mostly | nothing the Beacon | six rooms | ? | less than the first three

He looked at that page for a long time, and then he did the thing that would define his entire adult life, which was to take the problem seriously instead of being annoyed about it.

Because there were two possibilities, and only one of them was interesting.

Either the Tally was unfair, in which case there was nothing he could do about it except be angry, and anger is a poor fuel for a decade of work.

Or — and he turned this over for a long time before he let himself write it down — the Tally was correct, and his work was in the wrong column.

✦ ✦ ✦

The more he looked at it, the more certain he became, and the more it frightened him.

The field manual had twenty-four guides in it and three working tools, and it worked with no network at all, and it had been built for agents who were on the road and on night buses and needed an answer between one stop and the next. Two hundred of them were using it. He knew because the counter told him, and he had built the counter himself, and it was the only part of any of his work that anyone had ever audited.

And what did the Tally say?

Content delivery. Category: informational. Users: unverified. Commercial value: nil.

And it was not lying. It was a set of true statements, and the total of those true statements added up to almost nothing, and somewhere between the ledger and the form there was a gap where the actual value should have been, and the gap was not an error. The gap was a category.

He wrote in the margin of the ledger, in a hand that was not quite steady:

What do you call a thing that changes a person's week, if there is no name on the form for it?

He did not have an answer for two years. But he noticed, in that period, something else that sat badly with him, and he noticed it because it was a small thing and small things had never troubled him before.

The Beacon — his own unfinished machine, the thing he built to prove he could build machines — was the only work he had ever done that had never made him a single unit of brightwork. Not underpaid. Not poorly rated. Zero. On every single assessment, for three years, the entry had read no demonstrated return.

The Beacon had never saved anybody anything. It had never moved a single sack, filled a single order, or kept a single machine running. It had only ever demonstrated, to him, that he could build a thing that behaved the way he told it to.

He found that he could not decide how he felt about that, and that not being able to decide was the most uncomfortable feeling he had had in years.

✦ Field note · what actually happened

The portfolio site itself is the Beacon: a marketing site whose product is persuasion rather than a tool. It has never taken an order or filled a form that mattered to anyone but him. In the private notes he keeps, he calls it the thing he built to prove he could build things.

The honest, uncomfortable part of this chapter is real. Three of the five projects on that site have no revenue attached to them in any ledger whatsoever — the voucher app, the field manual, the reading plans were all given away. The site's testimonial copy is placeholder text, and two of its category filters lead to an empty grid, because the work exists but the categories for it were never filled in.

Act One · The Ash Chapter Six

A Name for the Machine

He had six rooms and one page, and he rebuilt it forty times, and every rebuild was an argument with himself.

The Beacon had six rooms.

The first was the Threshold — where a visitor arrived, and where they were told what he built, in a sentence that had to survive being read on a phone in one bar of signal.

The second was the Wall — the case studies, five of them, each one written the way a Brightwright should write: here is the problem, here is what was built, here is what changed. He wrote them long and honestly and then cut them in half, and then cut them in half again, and the reason he cut them was that he had discovered the fifth thing about audiences, which is that the only thing they will definitely read is the first sentence.

The third was the Ledger Room — the process, the guarantees, the plain statement that a client would own every line of what he wrote. He had originally promised a fourth thing here, which was testimonials from people who had been pleased with his work, and he had to take it out.

He took it out because he had two written statements from named people and no way on earth to make them true, and he would rather have an empty room than a furnished one.

The fourth room was the thing he was actually proud of, and the reason he had started. He called it the Counting Room, and it contained a machine that did arithmetic.

The fifth was the Threshold again, twice over — the ways in, the two doors at the bottom of the page, one to a live calendar and one to a direct line. Both of them were carefully built to be impossible to click into nothing.

The sixth was the smallest and, for a long time, his favourite: a set of letters that would not stay still.

✦ ✦ ✦

In the Threshold, the sentence was fixed for its first four words and then the rest of it kept changing. I build high-impact — and then a word would arrive, one letter at a time, and hold for two seconds, and then un-arrive, and the next one would come.

web apps. websites. mobile apps. APIs and services. automations. Over and over, all night, in front of whoever had walked in.

He had put those four fixed words in deliberately, and he had done it after a long argument with himself about whether the effect was a cheap trick.

His conclusion was this, and it is the single most useful thing he ever worked out in that room:

A visitor will decide about you in the four seconds before they read a single word. The animation is not a substitute for anything. It is a substitute for being available — for keeping the light on, one phrase at a time, all night, for the person who walks in at three in the morning.

And then he made the mistake that taught him the rest.

The first time he built it, the letters started as nothing. There was a moment — a real, visible moment, about a third of a second — where the sentence read I build high-impact and then a gap, and then the next word began to write itself, and during that gap every visitor in the world saw a man who had run out of things to say.

He caught it on the second night. He fixed it by making the very first word already there before anything moved, so that the machine was never empty, not even for a moment, not even at the start.

He would think about that gap for a very long time afterwards, because it was the first time in his life that he had understood something about people rather than about things. It was not that the animation had been too slow.

It was that he had designed for the second visitor and not the first one.

✦ Field note · what actually happened

All six rooms are real sections of the site: the hero, the case-study showcase, the process and guarantees, the scope estimator, the contact and booking hub, and the typing effect in the headline that cycles twelve specialities.

The empty-first-letter bug is real too. The typing effect originally started from an empty string, which meant every first-time visitor saw a blank gap. The fix was to make the first word the starting value — which is small, unglamorous, and the reason a visitor now never sees the site look broken. It is also the reason the server and the browser agree on what is on screen; get that wrong and the page flickers and throws errors.

Act One · The Ash Chapter Seven

The Courier's Refusal

The Sent had been trying to tell him something for three weeks, and he had not listened, and this chapter is about the moment he did.

Every piece of work Dammie finished had to be sent somewhere. That was the arrangement and it was not optional: a Brightwright built a thing, and the thing had to reach a person, and the person who carried it was called the Sent and wore a grey coat and was the most reliable creature on the coast.

The trouble arrived in the Counting Room.

A visitor could build a small machine in there — pick a shape, pick a depth, choose some extras — and the machine would give them a number, and then a visitor could send that machine to Dammie, and it would arrive in his inbox with the number on it, and the visitor's requirements with it.

Which meant the Beacon had a door in it that a stranger could open, and behind that door a message had to leave the building and arrive in Amara, and be readable, and be right.

✦ ✦ ✦

Dammie went to the depot on the east road to ask about the Sent, and he came away with something he did not understand and could not put down.

The courier system had a limit. Everybody knew about the limit; it was discussed constantly, the way people discuss the tide. A message could be addressed to anyone. A message could be sent by anyone. But it would only ever be delivered to the single address it was issued against, and everything else about it — every name, every reply, every conversation that followed — would loop back to that one place.

Dammie explained what he wanted. He wanted a visitor to be able to write to him and for the message to land in his own hand.

The clerk at the depot looked at him for a moment. Then she said the thing that took him three weeks to be able to hear.

It can already do that. And it will keep doing it. Here is the part you have not asked about: when your visitor writes to you, the reply goes to you. Not to them. You will read their message and answer it, and they will never see that you did, and in six months when you want to send them a receipt, or a reminder, or a second question, you will discover that the letterbox on this whole coast only has one name on it.

✦ ✦ ✦

He thought about it that night on the roof of the lean-to, and then he did the thing he always did, which was to go and find the smallest possible version of the problem.

So he made one small enquiry.

He sent a message to himself. Name, address, a category, a sentence. He watched it arrive. It arrived. It was exactly right. The Sent had taken it, carried it, and delivered it to the only letterbox on the coast and it was sitting in his inbox, correctly formatted, in about a second and a half.

And it worked. It worked every time. He could not break it, which should have been the answer.

What he took from that night was the sentence he repeated to himself for the next decade, and which eventually became the argument of this entire book:

Function Point Analysis is real, and the numbers here are the real ones. The Counting of Lines

Raising something to the power of more than one makes it grow faster than the thing you measured. So a project twice the size does not take twice as long. It takes more than twice as long. The people who built this rule four hundred years ago had noticed, in the ordinary way that people notice things, that everything gets harder faster than it gets bigger, and they had put a number on it.

Dammie wrote in the margin: the exponent is the whole point. It is not a fudge. It is the discovery that scale has a cost.

The factory system — the eight hundred and six stones, the twenty-one rooms — he had been building for years in the small hours, on his own, on the far end of a wharf.

The merchant's rule, applied to it, said it was about ninety-four months if he was entirely honest about the size of the thing, and the only way to get it under a year was to accept a completely different scope.

It was not wrong. ✦ Field note · what actually happened

The equation and all the constants are the real ones, straight out of the original research: Effort = a × KLOC^b, with a = 2.4, b = 1.05, c = 2.5, d = 0.38 for ordinary projects; 3.0 / 1.12 / 2.5 / 0.35 for mixed; 3.6 / 1.20 / 2.5 / 0.32 for difficult. The observation that b always exceeds 1 is real too — it encodes what the literature calls diseconomies of scale.

The successor model, COCOMO II, is the same idea with the crude parts removed: a calibration constant, an exponent that is adjusted by five rated factors (has this been done before, how free are you to change course, how much risk has already been removed, how capable is the team, how well do they know each other), and seventeen effort multipliers. It was recalibrated against 161 completed projects. Its own manual says plainly that for anything under a hundred people you probably do not need all seventeen.

The Threefold Guess

The fifth measure is three sentences long, costs nothing, and is worth more to Dammie than the other four put together.

Never give one number. Give three. The best you can honestly imagine. The one you actually expect. And the one you are afraid of — the bad one, the one where everything that normally goes wrong, goes wrong.

And do not average them. That is the whole trick, and it is the only trick. Do not average them equally, because the middle one is far more likely than either end. Give the middle one four votes.

The ruleWHAT YOU WILL PROBABLY NEED = (best + 4 × likely + worst) ÷ 6 HOW WRONG YOU MIGHT BE = (worst − best) ÷ 6 add ONE of those to the first number and you will be right about 84 times out of 100. add TWO and you will be right about 98 times out of 100. and you have just told the truth about how uncertain you are, out loud, before you start, which is the only moment it is cheap to say.

Three-point estimation is the most useful thing in this report. The formula (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6 is the weighted mean, the middle value gets four votes because it is far more likely than either extreme, and the standard deviation (P − O) ÷ 6 tells you how wide your uncertainty actually is.

Worked through a real example in the pricing document: ninety, a hundred and fifty, two hundred and eighty hours gives an expected one hundred and fifty-three, a standard deviation of about thirty-two, and therefore a quote of roughly one hundred and eighty-five hours to be right eighty-four per cent of the time. Same work, same builder, about a fifth more money.

The last paragraph is the real finding and it is not a formula. Dammie had been quoting the middle number deliberately, because the safe number felt dishonest to him. It is not dishonest. The most radical thing Dammie ever built was a document, and he built it by giving away the one thing the trade had always guarded hardest: the working.

He had learned something in that year about instruments that he had not expected, and it was this.

Every instrument he had used was downstream of somebody writing a number down where the next one could find it. Obi's five lists were four hundred years of people doing exactly that in the margin of their own work. Iyabo's instrument was forty thousand entries, most of them made by men she would never meet, in exchange for a percentage, because the alternative to being written down was not being paid at all.

And a measurement that cannot be checked by the person being measured is not a measurement. It is a verdict. The whole difference between the two, across nine months, turned out to be the difference between writing the record where the subject can read it and writing it where the subject cannot.

✦ ✦ ✦

So he wrote a book, and he wrote it badly on purpose.

Not the whole of it. He could not have written the whole of it. There were a hundred and eleven pages of Obi's in a hand that was not his, and a hundred and forty-one more of his own, and a year of small hours, and a family, and sleep, and he was not going to pretend otherwise.

He wrote the parts that could be written. The arithmetic. The rate he had been charging. The arithmetic that showed what the rate had actually been worth per hour. The scale that had been wrong by a quarter. The mistake with the key, in the first person, in the first paragraph, with the date on it. The five measures, as he had received them, with the attribution intact and the original spelling.

And he wrote all of it in the plainest language he could find, and then he made it readable by somebody who did not know what a Brightwright was.

What he told himself he was doing, in the notebookI am not going to be allowed to fix anything. I know that. I am not the Regrader and I do not have a chair and I will not be invited to the brass room again. So I am not going to argue with the instrument. I am going to write the working down, where everyone can read it, and I am going to put the errors in with the arguments, and I am going to explain every term, and then I am going to leave it where it can be found by whoever comes next. That is all I have. It is not nothing.

He sent it out into the world with no corrections and no second edition and no way of taking it back, which was deliberate, and the reason was the same as the reason he had put the key mistake in the first paragraph.

A record that can be quietly corrected by its author is not a record. It is an opinion with a date on it.

✦ ✦ ✦

And it went out further than anything he had ever sent anywhere.

Not because it was good — he was under no illusion about that, and he wrote the whole thing while worried it was not. It went out because it was useful, and because usefulness travels along one single line: somebody finds it, it helps them with something, they send it on to somebody else it will help, and nobody has to like it or believe it or even have read it.

A man in a port two thousand kilometres away, working on a system that counted containers, read the part about the fourth column and stopped what he was doing, and went and found out what his own system could not see.

A young woman on a coast with a different sky entirely read the part about the wrong rate and fixed the same error he had fixed, in the same week, without ever speaking to him.

A teacher somewhere put three of the five measures into a course, and then four, and then all five, and eleven hundred people who will never know his name now cost their work the way the coast of Amara decided it should be costed four hundred years ago, in a language that had been in a single head on a wharf for a decade.

I spent my whole life believing the work had to be measured before it was worth anything. I did not occur to me that writing down the measuring was itself the work, and the only part of it that could leave the coast.

He never got a Tally entry for it. There was no column for it, and there never had been, and he had stopped waiting for one.

What he had instead was the only instrument on that coast that anybody could pick up, read, argue with, correct, and use without asking anybody for permission.

Act Three · The Column Chapter Twenty-Eight

Brightwork

He went back to the salt shed to look at a latch he had built when he was nine years old, and he understood, at last, what he had been looking at all his life.

It was still there. Of course it was. Nobody on the Ninth Wharf fixes a door out of sentiment; they fix it because a bent latch is a bent latch, and it had been fixed by somebody, and it worked, and it had gone on working for a generation under a constant stream of men who had no idea at all who had put it there.

He stood and looked at it for a long time, and the light came off it — the good light, the bright kind, the kind that climbs — and it went up about four feet into the air of a salt shed at the poor end of the harbour and stopped there, as it had every day for twenty-odd years, doing nobody in particular an enormous amount of invisible good.

When I was nine I learned to tell two kinds of light apart, and I have told that story twenty times, and every single time I have told it as though it was about machines.

It was never about the machines.

The bright kind came off things that did what they were supposed to do. The ash kind came off things that only looked like they did. And I spent nine years learning to tell those two apart in my own work — which is the harder version of the same test — and I got it wrong at the exact point where it mattered most, because I was measuring the glow and never once asking who was standing in it.

✦ ✦ ✦

He understood, finally, that the two kinds of light were not a property of the thing at all.

They were a property of the relationship between the thing and the person it was for. A machine with nobody waiting on it has no colour. A machine that somebody has been quietly relying on for three years without either of them noticing is the brightest thing on the coast, and the instrument cannot see it, and there is no version of the instrument that will.

The salt-shed latch: four hundred men a day for twenty years, and not one of them ever thought about it, and every single one of them a little bit freer for having it.

A shift worker on a phone with one bar, doing arithmetic at eleven at night instead of losing a month's pay over a day that somebody else wrote down wrongly.

Two hundred field agents who are not, for the first time in their lives, the only person in the room who knows something.

An inspector who looks at a number every morning and goes and stands by a silo.

✦ ✦ ✦

And he understood why Iyabo had held that chair for nineteen years, and why the clerk had put his four lines at the front of the book, and why a man at the end of a wharf had kept a copy of four hundred years of other people's arithmetic in a salt-warped case under a bed.

Because the whole of the coast's work is done in the dark, by people, for other people, and it only glows on the other side, and the one thing in four hundred years that nobody has found a way to do is to be in two places at once — the person who builds it and the person it was built for.

So you write it down. That is all that is left. You write down the number, and the working, and the mistakes, in a place where the person it is about can read it, and then you stop, because that is the entire distance between one person and the next one, and it has always been the entire distance.

✦ ✦ ✦

He went home along the wharf at about two in the morning, which was when the Beacon was worth visiting, because that was when it was busiest.

The Threshold came up. The letters began to write themselves, one at a time, at the speed of somebody typing, and the sentence built itself in front of him the way it had every night for three years:

Two in the morning, on the Ninth WharfI build high-impact web apps. websites. mobile apps. APIs and services. automations. and it went on, and it did not stop, and it had never once stopped, and it would still be there at four, and at six, and for whoever came in at seven, and for whoever came in at three the following morning.

He stood in the six metres of lean-to and watched the light off his own work come up the walls, and it was the bright kind.

Not because it had grown, and not because it was worth more, and not because anybody down at the Tally had changed a single figure on a single form. It was the bright kind because there was somebody standing in it.

There is only ever one test, and it has been in front of us since we were nine years old, and it is not whether the light climbs. It is whether anybody is standing in it when it does.

He closed the door. He had work in the morning, and there were four hundred people on the coast with a number on their own form that they had set once and never looked at again, and the whole point of a public record is that it does not go anywhere while you sleep.

The letters kept arriving, one at a time, all night, in a building he had built himself, on a coast eleven kilometres of flat grey water from anywhere, for whoever walked in.

I hid the real thing inside a made-up one, and the Field Notes are where I take the mask off.

There is no coast called Amara. There is no light that rises off a solved problem, no Tally, no Requisition Store, and no assessor with a soft voice and a very good instrument. All of that is invented, and I enjoyed inventing it enormously, and I hope some of it was a surprise.

Everything under each Field Note is not invented.

A factory where the record was kept on paper and the silos emptied themselves at night. A shift worker losing a month of pay over one miscounted day, on a phone with one bar of signal. A field manual buried in a chat group. A single machine that took down an entire build because of one key, in one corner, on one long night. A preview that would not play and a woman of nine who explained it better than an adult could have. A rate that had been wrong for two years and a stranger in a taxi who was the only person who ever checked it. The discovery that a number nobody complains about is a number you have never tested.

All of those are mine, and all of them happened, and I want to be plain about the ratio: the fiction is on top, but the thing underneath it is a record of about two years of building, and roughly forty nights of not sleeping, and one small, genuine, sustained piece of stubbornness about doing it properly.

Why I dressed it up like this

Because a case study is not a story, and stories travel. If I had written a page explaining that my build process collided with an environment variable and I fixed it, you would have skimmed it and closed the tab, and you would have been right to.

Nobody on this coast has ever heard of a Brightwright who could not make his machine work eventually. But everybody has met a man who could not make his machine work, and who kept going anyway, at three in the morning, in a room that is too small, with nobody watching. That person is easier to recognise, and easier to learn something from, because you are not being taught a technology — you are being shown a person deciding what to do next.

That was the bet. If it worked, you would have learned nine years of engineering by the time you got to the end, and you would not have had to learn a single framework, and you would have recognised a good deal of it in your own work.

If you are a builder, there is a method in here

The five lists in the middle of this book are not decorative. They are real methods for costing software work, and they are the reason the last part of the story has a happy ending, because they are the only reason it can have one at all:

Count your deeds
Break the work into finished things, add the testing, then add everything that is not coding. Most of a project is not the part people picture.
Count function, not code
Measure what a person can do with the finished thing. The standard, the weight tables and a worked example are in the app documentation.
Guess three times
Best, likely, and worst — then weight the middle one four times. The cheapest accuracy improvement in the trade, and worth about a fifth of your price.
Price the keeping
Building a thing is only 20–35% of what it costs to own. The rest has no line item, which is why nobody charges for it — and why nobody should give it away.
Check your rate
Not once a year. Once a month. A conversion you set a long time ago is the single most common quiet error in this whole trade, and only one person I have ever worked for noticed it.
Write it down
In a place the person it is about can read it. This is the whole of chapter twenty-seven, and it is the only part of the method that outlives you.

And if you are about to start something

Dammie spent nine years believing his work was worth more, and the thing that actually cost him those nine years was not a shortage of skill. It was that he had not written down what an hour of his life was worth, so he never knew what his own work was worth, so he could not tell whether anyone was shortchanging him or whether he was simply guessing.

The instruments in this book take about an afternoon each to learn and about a year of writing things down to use properly. That year of writing things down is the only price of admission. It is also the only part of the whole craft that nobody can take from you.

If you take one thing out of this, take the notebook. Not the method. The notebook. Start recording what things actually take you, today, because that is the whole of it, and because in three years it will be the only instrument you own that nobody else has.

And build things for people who cannot afford the obvious answer. That is where the work is, and it always has been, and nobody is going to send you a brief for it.

Glossary of the world

Every strange word in this book, explained the way it was explained to him.

The coast

Amara
The grey-coast city where the story happens. Invented, except for the coastline.
The Ninth Wharf
The poor end of the harbour: three cranes, a salt shed, a customs house with a leak in the roof, and a tin shed where assessments happen.
The Beacon
Dammie's machine. Six rooms, one page, endlessly rebuilt. The thing he built to prove he could build machines.
Brightwork
The light a finished thing gives off when it genuinely works. The substance of the whole book, and the thing that is hard to measure.
Ash
The grey, dead glow off a thing that only looks like it works. Indistinguishable from dirt until far too late.
Brightwright
A builder. From "bright" and "wright", an old word for a maker. Dammie's trade and his surname's meaning.
The Book
The published record of the whole machine — every line, every note, every version. Readable by anyone. This is why the key in chapter nineteen mattered so much.
The Sent
The courier in the grey coat. Will carry a message anywhere; will only ever deliver to the one address it was issued against.

The instruments

The Tally
Four hundred years of measurement on this coast. Not a market — a record. It records what it has seen, and it has never pretended otherwise.
Value
What the Tally declares a finished thing is worth, from three questions: how big, what does it do, how long will it keep doing it.
The rule
The mechanical successor to the Assessors. Deterministic, impartial, fast, and completely reliable at measuring one category of value in the entire world.
The Requisition Store
A trench lined with a substance that holds a signal without losing it. Anything, from anywhere, on demand. The thing that reorganised the whole coast's economy in nine minutes.
A Regrader
The office that maintains the rule. Iyabo held the second chair of Amara for nineteen years and did not once pretend the instrument was whole.
A measure
One of five ways to put an honest number on a piece of work. Four of them predate the personal computer. All five are real and still in use.
A stone
The unit of the second measure — one thing a person can do with a finished machine. You can carry it, and you cannot argue with it once it is on the scale.
The fourth question
Dammie's discovery: not how big, not what it does, not how long — but who would be worse off if it did not exist, and by how much, in a unit of their own. Nobody measures it, because it is full of absences.

Terms that appear in the Field Notes

These are real and current, and they are explained here so that nothing in the notes needs a second document to understand.

Prerender
Building a page into finished HTML before anybody asks for it. Fast, and the reason the Beacon's front door opens instantly — and the reason some of its settings can only be changed at build time.
Runtime vs build time
A setting read while the thing is running, versus one read while it is being made. Chapter seventeen is entirely about the difference and why it is so easy to get wrong.
A facade
Showing a still image and a play button instead of the real thing, and loading the real thing only when someone asks. Saves a megabyte and a set of tracking requests for people who were never going to watch.
Module scope
Code that runs once, when a file is opened, rather than when it is used. The single most dangerous place to put anything that can fail — it takes down everything, not just the thing that broke.
A secret
A credential held in a store you can write to and cannot read back. Write-only by design. Never in a published file, ever.
TCO
Total Cost of Ownership — what a thing costs across its whole life, not just the build. Between three and five times the build price.
ISO 20926
The international standard for counting function, which is the second measure in this book, and which is recognised in formal software contracts today.
A person-month
One person working for one month. The research model treats it as about 152 hours. Not the same as a calendar month.
The exponent above one
The reason the crudest measure in the trade still works: it encodes the discovery that scale has a cost, so bigger projects take disproportionately longer.
