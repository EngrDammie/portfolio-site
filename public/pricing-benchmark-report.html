I The landscape

01 · Why this report exists

The question behind the question.

You did not ask "what is a website worth". You asked something more practical:

"Am I pricing my work fairly — not so low that I am cheating myself, and not so high that I lose every client to someone cheaper?"

That is the right question, and it cannot be answered from inside your own head. Every developer believes their price is reasonable. The only way to know is to compare against what the market actually does — and that comparison has to use the same units, or it is useless.

You then asked a second, sharper question: whether there is an established formula or convention for costing a piece of software work. There is, and it is older and more formal than most freelancers realise. Part IV covers five established methods in full, with the actual equations, the weight tables, the standard constants, and worked examples you can copy.

The three traps in this kind of research

⚠ The vendor's own number

Most cost guides are written by companies that sell the thing being priced. They are not lying, but they have every reason to make the market look expensive. This report leans on surveys of many firms and on live marketplace data wherever that exists.

⚠ Comparing across currencies

A dollar figure for a US developer and a dollar figure for a Nigerian developer are not the same product. Region alone moves prices by a factor of eight. Section 06 handles this properly.

⚠ Prices that are not prices

Some published figures are sales pages. One firm publicly lists a $599 single-page site; another lists a $38,000 "simple app". Both are real. Neither is comparable unless you know what was inside.

Your website price is defensible and genuinely competitive. It will win you work against local competition and it is not insulting to anyone. Undercharging is not generosity — it is a slow business failure. A client who gets a $900 mobile app assumes a $900 mobile app, and will not pay for a second one. Someone who gets everything for almost nothing does not feel grateful; they feel lucky, and they negotiate the next project too. And once the market learns your time is cheap, reversing that is very difficult.

04 · Why prices vary so much

Five factors explain almost all of the spread you will see in the data.

  1. Where the developer is. By a distance the biggest factor. The same senior developer bills about $150/hour in San Francisco and about $40/hour in Bucharest or Buenos Aires. Most pricing guides note the quality difference is "often negligible" — which is exactly why one project can legitimately cost three times as much in two places.
  2. Who the client is. A US enterprise with a compliance officer and a procurement process pays for risk transfer, not just code. A founder bootstrapping a side project pays less because they have less budget, not because the work differs.
  3. How the work is sold. There is a consistent premium running upwards: marketplace cheapest, then freelance direct, then dedicated team, then agency. UK data puts platform rates 20–40% below direct rates; India data puts agency at roughly 2× a dedicated developer, because you pay for project management, QA and sales too.
  4. What "simple" means to whoever is quoting. The biggest single cause of the wild spread between published figures. One source's "simple app" is $15,000; another's is $38,000. Both are honest descriptions of different things.
  5. How much of the sale is risk. The most underestimated cost in software is not writing code — it is being accountable for it. A firm that guarantees the result, or absorbs a failed launch, must charge for that certainty. A developer who personally owns the outcome can charge less for identical code.
“The clearest published breakdown of where a software budget actually goes comes from a mobile app cost analysis: backend 26%, frontend 22%, design 16%, QA 14%, project management 12%, DevOps 10%. Only front and back end together is under half the bill — so a quote built purely on how long the coding takes will always come out too low, because it silently omits five of the six categories.

The most trustworthy number in the industry, because it is the hardest to dress up.

Even if you never charge by the hour, hourly rates still matter to you. If you divide what a project costs by the hours it really took, the answer is an hourly rate. It is the honest way to check whether your own prices make sense.

What actually separates a cheap website from an expensive one

Not the number of pages. Every source that breaks this down lands on the same three levers.

1️⃣ Made from scratch, or from a template

If someone takes a ready-made design and heavily adjusts it, that is $1,500–$4,000. If every screen is designed individually, that is $4,000–$15,000+. This one choice can be a five-times difference.

2️⃣ What it can actually do

A page that just tells people about you, and a website where people can book, pay and sign in, are not the same project. One UK source puts custom applications at £10,000+ versus £2,000–£5,000 for a basic information site.

3️⃣ Cost per extra page

Several firms publish this plainly: about $99–$299 for each extra page beyond the starting package. Fifteen pages is not a five-page site with more words on it.

What genuinely moves a mobile app's price

📱 One platform or two?

Building separate versions for iPhone and Android roughly doubles the front-end work. Using one codebase for both is 30–45% cheaper and covers most apps.

⚙ Each extra feature

Login beyond a simple email, buying things inside the app, push messages, working offline, camera or location, live updates, an admin screen — each one adds 15–30% to the total.

🗄 The hidden half

About half the budget is not the app screens at all. It is the servers, database and connections behind them. "A form that emails you is cheap" — everything else is not.

And what it costs to keep alive afterwards

Sources are consistent here: expect to spend 15–20% of the build cost every single year keeping an app running and up to date. Publishing costs are small and fixed: about $99 a year for Apple, and roughly $25 once for Google Play.

Three things that decide which band you land in

  1. Is it a script or a brain? A rule-based bot is a decision tree — cheap and predictable. Anything using a language model is more expensive, because it can do more and can also go wrong in more ways.
  2. How many systems does it touch? One workflow with one connection is the cheapest useful thing to build. Reaching into a CRM, a calendar, documents and operating software at once multiplies the work.
  3. Who carries the risk of a wrong answer? A system that drafts a reply for a human to check is cheap. One that sends money or changes records on its own is not, because it needs testing, permissions and safety checks.
💸
The cost people forget: running the AI

Every AI system costs money every month to operate, on top of building it. Published 2026 model pricing runs from about $0.25–$3.00 per million input tokens for lighter models up to $12–$15 per million output tokens for the strongest. Building the system is the smaller half of the bill if it runs at all.

IV The estimating methods

This is the part you asked for: is there an established formula for putting a number on a piece of software work? Yes — five of them, and some are nearly fifty years old. They are written out below in plain language, with the real equations, the weight tables and worked examples you can copy.

21 · Why formulas exist

The reason a chef weighs ingredients instead of guessing.

Two people estimating the same project by feel will differ by a factor of two. That is not because one is lying — it is because both are guessing. Software estimation has been studied since the 1960s precisely because intuitive estimating is unreliable, and the result is a set of standard, repeatable methods.

They all exist to answer the same three questions, in this order:

  1. How big is the work? Not in hours — hours are an output, not an input. In something that does not change when you change your mind, or your language, or your team.
  2. How long will it take, and how much does that cost? Turning size into effort, then effort into money.
  3. How sure am I? Every estimate is a range. The method exists to tell you how wide.
★ Why "size" cannot be measured in hours

If you size a project in hours, you have already guessed the answer — you just wrote it down earlier. If you size it in lines of code, the number changes completely depending on which language you use: one behaviour might be five lines or fifty. All the formal methods therefore size work in something language-independent — most commonly in function points, which measure what the software does for its users.

23 · Method 1: bottom-up hours

The oldest method, still the most accurate for small projects.

Break the project into every individual task, estimate each one, add them up, then add a buffer. It sounds obvious, and that is its weakness: it is only as good as your task list, and a task you forgot to list contributes nothing.

How to do it

  1. List the deliverables, not the activities. "Contact page" is a deliverable. "Coding" is not.
  2. Break each into tasks of a few hours each. Anything larger hides unknowns.
  3. Estimate each task in hours, including the time to test it.
  4. Add the tasks up. This is your base effort.
  5. Add the non-coding tasks: discovery, design, testing, deployment, handover, and client communication time. Sources put these at roughly 30–40% of the total.
  6. Add a buffer — see section 29.
  7. Multiply by your loaded rate — see section 28.

24 · Method 2: Function Point Analysis

The international standard, and the one that holds up in front of a client.

Function Point Analysis was invented at IBM in the late 1970s by Allan Albrecht and is now an ISO standard. It is the closest thing this industry has to a shared measuring unit for software, and it is recognised in formal contracts worldwide.

💡 The idea in one sentence

Measure the software by what it lets the user do, not by how much code it took to write. Two developers in two countries writing the same product in different languages get the same function point count — and therefore the same price.

Step 1 — Count five kinds of function

An elementary process is the smallest thing a user does that is meaningful to them: "add a record", "view a list", "change a setting". You classify each one into five types.

ILF — Internal Logical FileA group of data the system holds and manages itselfYour customers table; the products database
EIF — External Interface FileA group of data held by another system that you readPayment provider data; a third-party API feed

Each function is rated Low (1), Average (2) or High (3) based on how many data elements it touches and how many files it reads or writes. You then look up the weight in this table.

Add up every weighted function. That total is the Unadjusted Function Point count, written as UFP.

Step 3 — Adjust for complexity of the whole system

Some systems are harder to build than their raw function count suggests, because of things like performance demands, how hard the data is to reach across locations, or how often it must be changed. You rate 14 characteristics from 0 to 5, add them up, and convert:

The value adjustment factor
TDI = sum of all 14 ratings        (0–5 each, so 0 to 70)
VAF = 0.65 + (0.01 × TDI)          (ranges from 0.65 to 1.35)
Adjusted FP = UFP × VAF

The 14 characteristics are:

Data communication · distributed data processing · performance demands · heavily used configuration · transaction role · online data entry · end-user efficiency · online update · complex processing · reusability · installation ease · operational ease · multiple sites · facilitate future change

A typical project lands on a TDI of around 35, giving VAF = 1.00 — which is exactly why the adjustment exists: it is designed to average out to "no change".

Step 4 — Turn function points into hours, using your own number

This is the step that decides everything, and there is only one correct source: your own measured productivity. You need a figure meaning "how many hours does it take me to deliver one function point?" Track it across three or four projects and use the average.

25 · Method 3: COCOMO II

The NASA model, built on 161 real projects.

COCOMO stands for Constructive Cost Model. The original was published by Dr Barry Boehm in 1981 and COCOMO II was recalibrated against 161 completed projects. It is the most thoroughly researched cost model ever built, and it is what a government or large enterprise expects to see in a formal bid.

The simple version first — the original COCOMO

Worth knowing because it is easy to remember and still roughly right for small work.

Basic COCOMO — two lines of arithmetic
Effort (person-months) = a × (KLOC) ^ b
Duration (months)   = c × (Effort) ^ d
Team size              = Effort ÷ Duration

KLOC means thousands of lines of code. The letters a, b, c, d are fixed constants that depend only on the kind of project:

Project typeabcdWhat it means
Organic2.41.052.50.38Small team, familiar problem, clear requirements. This is most client work.
Semi-detached3.01.122.50.35Medium team, mixed experience, some new tools
Embedded3.61.202.50.32Strict hardware or regulatory constraints

Source: COCOMO Quick Reference, PC2544 Software Effort Estimation course.

Note that b is always slightly above 1. That is software diseconomies of scale — bigger projects get harder faster than linearly.

The full version — COCOMO II

This is the one that replaced the original, and it is what you will see referenced.

COCOMO II — the two published equations
Effort (person-months) = A × (Size) ^ E × (EM1 × EM2 × ... × EMn)
Duration (months)   = C × (Effort) ^ SE

where   A  = 2.94   the calibration constant
        Size = KSLOC or unadjusted function points
        E   = B + (0.01 × sum of scale factors),  where B = 0.91
        C   = 3.67   the schedule constant
        SE  = D + 0.2 × (E − 0.91),  where D = 0.28
        EFi = the effort multipliers you rated for this project

Person-months convert at roughly 152 hours each.

The five scale factors

These modify the exponent, and they are what make COCOMO II smarter than the original. Each is rated on a six-point scale from Very Low to Extra High.

  1. Precedentedness — has this team built something like this before? The single biggest factor. Low precedent pushes the exponent up sharply.
  2. Development flexibility — how free are you to change course? Fixed requirements score better than vague ones.
  3. Architecture and risk resolution — how much uncertainty has already been removed? A well-agreed design scores far better than none.
  4. Personnel capability — how capable and experienced is the team?
  5. Team cohesion — do the people know each other, and have they worked together before?

E ranges from 0.91 to about 1.23 depending on these five ratings. A typical project with everything rated Nominal lands at E = 1.0997.

The effort multipliers

Unlike the scale factors, these multiply the answer rather than the exponent. There are 7 of them if you are estimating before the architecture exists, and 17 of them if you are estimating after. They cover the same ground as the 14 function point characteristics: product complexity, platform constraints, tool support, personnel capability and experience, and project factors.

26 · Method 4: story points and velocity

The modern method, and the one that suits ongoing work best.

A story point is a unit of relative size, not time. A "3" does not mean three days. It means "about as big as the things we called 3 before".

The scale

Teams use a Fibonacci-like scale, because the gaps grow: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34. The reason is deliberate — it reflects that going from 5 to 8 is a bigger relative jump than 1 to 2, and a scale that grows too evenly would be pretending to more precision than we have.

1–2 Tiny

Hours, not days. A typo, a colour change, a small fix.

3–5 Small

Under a day or two. One simple form, one screen.

8 Medium

A few days. A whole feature with its server side.

13 Large

About a week. Should be split, honestly.

21+ Too big

Must be broken into pieces. If it is one story, the estimate is fiction.

? Unknown

Spike it first — spend an hour researching, then re-estimate.

Velocity, and the formula that uses it

Velocity is simply how many points your team actually finished in the last sprint. It is measured, not predicted.

The velocity formula
Time to finish = (Points still to do) ÷ (Velocity × Confidence factor)

  90% confident  →  × 0.85
  80% confident  →  × 0.80

27 · Method 5: three-point estimation (PERT)

The cheapest accuracy improvement available to you.

Instead of giving one guess, give three — and the formula produces a better answer than any of them, plus an honest measure of your own uncertainty.

The PERT formulas
Expected effort = (Optimistic  +  4 × Most likely  +  Pessimistic) ÷ 6

Standard deviation = (Pessimistic − Optimistic) ÷ 6

Why the 4? It is the weighted mean — most likely outcomes are far more
common than the extremes, so they get four times the weight.

Expected effort = (90 + 4×150 + 280) ÷ 6 = 153 hours
Standard deviation = (280 − 90) ÷ 631.7 hours

The difference between the two is only about 20% — and it is the difference between a developer who keeps their promises and one who does not.

It is also worth roughly a fifth more money for the same work — and it is exactly how a client is meant to be treated.

28 · Loaded rates and the billable-hours trap

The most commonly ignored step in the whole calculation.

Almost everyone divides their hours by a number they invented. The number you need is called a loaded rate: what you must charge per hour of invoiced work to end up with the income you need.

The loaded rate formula
Loaded rate = ( Income you need  +  Business expenses  +  Tax)
              ÷ ( Billable hours per year  ×  (1 − tax rate) )

An easier, more accurate route — build it up from the bottom:

Loaded rate = (Target take-home + expenses) ÷ (1 − tax%) ÷ billable hours

The billable-hours problem, which is the real trap

You do not get paid for every hour you work. Studies of freelance earnings repeatedly find that only 50–65% of working hours ever turn into an invoice. The rest goes on:

  • Writing proposals and answering enquiries that lead nowhere
  • Marketing, your portfolio, keeping up with new tools
  • Invoicing, chasing payment, admin and record-keeping
  • Learning — which is unavoidable in this industry
  • Sick days, holidays, and the gaps between projects
⚠
The realistic figure

The most commonly cited working assumption is 25 billable hours a week across 48 working weeks = 1,200 billable hours a year — not 2,080. An employee gets roughly 125 billable hours a week; a freelancer without a full diary gets about 25. If you assume 40, every rate you calculate will be around 40% too optimistic.

29 · Buffers and contingency

The part of the estimate that pays for what you have not thought of yet.

A buffer is not padding. It is the honest allowance for work you could not have known about when you wrote the estimate. Every project has some; the only question is whether you priced it in or absorbed it yourself.

How much to add

Requirements fully written down and agreed in writing10–15%You know what you are building. Little room for surprises.

Client still deciding what they want30–50%Or do not quote a fixed price at all — quote a discovery phase first.
Integrating with an unfamiliar third-party system20–30%Other people's documentation is rarely complete.
App store or payment provider involved20–30%Compliance rules and review queues are outside your control.

Where your own promise leaves you exposed

You offer a 30-day warranty and weekly async updates. Both are good, and both should be priced. The warranty is roughly 1–2 days of unbilled time per project, and the updates are an hour a week for the project's life. At your current rates, neither is visible in the number.

✓
The two questions to ask before every quote

One. If this project takes 50% longer than I think, do I still make money? Two. If the client asks for one extra change, do I absorb it or is it already in the price? If the answer to either is no, the buffer is too small — raise it in the quote, not in your head.

30 · Total cost of ownership

The most commercially important idea in this report.

31 · Raise the price you get, not the number

Five ways to earn more without touching the estimator.

If you would rather not change the calculator, you still have room. These five changes alter what you earn without altering a single number a visitor sees.

  1. Stop the bleeding: charge for support. A written monthly plan covering maintenance, small changes and a response time. Clients expect to pay for this and currently get it free from you. This is the highest-value change on the list.
  2. Price by outcome, on the bigger jobs. Ask what the work is worth to the client, then charge a fair share. Covered properly in section 32.
  3. Sell more to the international market. The evidence is that billing overseas clients earns about 57% more per hour than billing locally, for identical work. You already serve the UK, US and Europe in your contact section — price those conversations in dollars.
  4. Get paid for discovery. A paid discovery phase removes the guesswork that makes fixed prices dangerous. Some sources credit the discovery cost back to the build price when the project proceeds, so the risk is genuinely removed.
  5. Write down what things actually take you. Start recording hours per project type today. In six months you will have a real productivity figure, and that is worth more than any number in this document.
📌
One more that costs nothing

Publish your rates instead of hiding them. A developer with visible pricing is trusted more, wastes less of your time on unqualified enquiries, and attracts clients who have already accepted the number. Published pricing is standard across the industry — several sources in this report are agencies that do exactly that.

32 · Pricing by value

The alternative to pricing by hours, and when it applies.

Everything in Part IV prices by effort. That is correct for most work, and it has one weakness: it cannot tell the difference between software that saves a business ten thousand a year and software that saves it a million. The hours are identical.

Value-based pricing fixes that by setting the fee from what the work is worth to the buyer, rather than what it costs you to produce.

The 10× rule

The most commonly cited version

Charge roughly 10% of the value the client receives. If a project is worth $50,000 to the business, a $5,000 fee leaves them with $45,000 of the upside and you with a project worth doing.

A second common version uses 10–20% of the first year's value. Sources that advocate this report that freelancers using it earn around 42% more per project than those billing hourly, and that the small group of consultants using it close $10,000+ projects at 31% higher rates.

When it works, and when it does not

✓ Value pricing works well
  • Automation that saves staff time
  • A site that generates leads or sales
  • Anything replacing a measurable cost
  • Repeat work with a known error rate
✗ Value pricing does not work
  • Requirements genuinely unknown
  • The result cannot be measured
  • Internal tools with no revenue link
  • One-off bespoke work with no repeating pattern

Worked example, using your own case studies

ProjectStated client impactValue pricing at 10%Your price today
Order processing automation Saves 2 hours 50 minutes of reconciliation per day — roughly 700 hours a year At even $30/hour of staff time saved, that is ~$21,000/yr → $2,100 per year of fee Web app, $1,500–$3,500 once
Recharge card formatter 3 hours of manual work reduced to under 5 seconds, with zero misprints on redeemable stock Higher still — it removes a costly error class Web app, $1,500–$3,500 once

The first example is instructive. The testimonial on your own site says the work saves about three hours a day. Priced purely on hours, that project is $2,000. Priced on the value it returns, it is a recurring annual saving for the client — and a supporting plan becomes easy to justify.

⚠
Two rules for doing this properly

One. Get the number from the client, not from yourself. Ask what the problem costs them today. If they cannot name it, value pricing is guesswork with a confident voice.

Two. Never show the hourly arithmetic behind a value price. The moment a client sees "20 hours at $100", they negotiate to "just 10 hours". A value price is one number tied to an outcome, and it stops being a negotiation about time.

33 · Find your own floor rate

The number below which you should not accept work, whatever the project.

A floor rate is the hourly rate below which a project is not worth taking. It exists because some jobs are unprofitable no matter how interesting they are — the discovery call that turns into a forty-hour build, or the client who asks for a fifth revision.

Your floor rate
Floor rate = (Minimum acceptable income + expenses) 
             ÷ billable hours
             ×  (1 ÷ risk factor)

reflecting the projects that overrun. Then take the market
rate for your region as a cross-check.

The three numbers to keep in your head

1 Your floor

Below this, say no. Protects you from the projects that quietly cost you money. For a developer in your region aiming to be paid properly, this tends to land somewhere in the $25–40/hour range before risk loading.

2 Your target

Where you want most work to sit. For you, that should be at or above the $28–45/hour mid-level band for Nigeria, Ghana and Kenya — and you should be able to reach $40–80 for direct international clients.

3 Your stretch

For the work you most want. Scarcity and speciality justify it: AI and LLM work commands $100–200+ in the US market. This is the rate for the flagship project, not the one for the routine website.

✓
What this all comes down to

Your target rate for a routine website is about $45/hour. Your target for a routine mobile app should be similar or higher, not a third of it. And your AI work — the highest-paid specialism in the entire industry — should be your most expensive line, not your cheapest. That is the whole argument of this report in one sentence.

35 · Pricing words explained

Every term used in this report, in ordinary language.

The estimating words

Effort
The work needed, measured in hours or person-months. An output of your estimate, never an input to it.
Size
How big the work is, measured in something that does not change with language or team — usually function points.
Function point
A standard unit measuring one thing a user can do with a piece of software. One point is worth a few hours.
UFP
Unadjusted Function Points — the raw total before you account for the overall system's complexity.
VAF
Value Adjustment Factor. Scales the raw count up or down (0.65 to 1.35) for things like performance demands or how often it must change.
Productivity
How many function points you personally deliver per hour. This is your number, measured — never borrowed.
KLOC
Thousands of Lines Of Code. The old way of sizing software, used by the original COCOMO model.
Person-month
One person working for one month. COCOMO II treats this as roughly 152 hours.
Exponent
The power a number is raised to in a formula. In COCOMO it rises when a project is riskier, which makes the estimate grow faster than the size.
Effort multiplier
A factor above or below 1.00 that adjusts effort for a rated risk. Above 1 means more work than average; below 1 means less.
Story point
A relative unit of size in agile work. A 3 is "about as big as the things we called 3" — it is not a number of days.
Velocity
How many story points a team actually finished in the last sprint. Measured, not predicted — the only truly honest number in any method here.
PERT
Program Evaluation and Review Technique. Gives three estimates — optimistic, most likely, pessimistic — and combines them into one better number plus a measure of uncertainty.
Loaded rate
What you must charge per billable hour to end up with the income you need, after expenses and tax.
Billable hours
Hours that actually become an invoice. Usually 1,200 a year, not 2,080 — the rest goes on proposals, admin, learning and gaps.
Buffer / contingency
Money or time allowed for what you have not thought of yet. 10–15% when requirements are written down, 20–30% when they are only discussed.
TCO
Total Cost of Ownership — the whole cost over the life of something, not just the build. Usually 3–5× the build price.

The commercial words

MVP
Minimum Viable Product — the smallest version that genuinely works and can be tested with real users. Not a broken full product.
SaaS
Software as a Service — a web application people pay a subscription for, rather than one they buy once.
Multi-tenant
One system serving many separate customers, with their data fully kept apart. It is an architecture decision, not a feature you add later.
API
A way for one piece of software to ask another for data or actions. Integrating one is real work with real failure modes.
Ecommerce
Selling directly online: catalogue, basket, payment, shipping. Far more expensive than an information website.
Retainer
A recurring monthly fee for ongoing work, rather than a one-off payment.
Value-based pricing
Charging a share of what the work is worth to the client, rather than a share of the hours it takes you.
Effective hourly rate
A project price divided by the hours it really took. The most useful number in this entire report.
Floor rate
The rate below which a job is not worth accepting, whatever else it might offer.
Scope creep
Work gradually being added to a project without the price changing. The single largest cause of freelance projects losing money.
Discovery
The work done before building: agreeing what is needed, why, and what it costs. Roughly 10% of a project's budget, and the best protection against a wrong estimate.
Parallel / black market rate
The naira exchange rate outside official channels. Usually slightly higher than the official rate, which is the rate that matters for your pricing.
Offshore / nearshore
Contracting a team in a cheaper country. "Offshore" means far away in time zone; "nearshore" means only a few hours apart.
